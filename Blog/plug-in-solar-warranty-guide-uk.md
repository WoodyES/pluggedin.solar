With plug-in solar going legal on [27 August 2026](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk), thousands of people are about to buy their first kit within days of each other. Most of that attention is going on wattage, price and mounting options — understandably, since those are the numbers on the box. Almost nobody is reading the warranty terms before they check out, which is a mistake, because the warranty is the thing that decides whether a failed panel in year three is a five-minute phone call or a written-off £500.

Warranty terms for plug-in microgenerator kits are not standardised the way the electrical spec is. The 800VA / 3.5A output limit, BS 1363 plug and 5A fuse requirements under [SI 2026 No. 848](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk) are fixed by law. What happens when a panel degrades faster than expected, or a micro-inverter dies, is entirely down to the manufacturer's own terms — and those vary a lot between the brands stocked at [B&Q, Currys, Amazon, Screwfix, Wickes and Lidl](/blog/retailers-stocking-plug-in-solar-uk-2026).

## The three warranties bundled into one kit

A plug-in solar kit isn't one product with one warranty — it's three components, each typically covered separately:

**The solar panel itself.** This usually carries the longest warranty, often 10–25 years, but it's split into two different promises. A *product warranty* (typically 10–12 years) covers manufacturing defects — delamination, frame corrosion, junction box failures. A separate *performance warranty* guarantees the panel won't degrade below a stated output threshold (commonly 80–87% of rated output) over 25 years. These are not the same thing, and a panel can be well within its performance warranty while being outside its product warranty for a physical fault.

**The micro-inverter or optimiser.** This is the component that actually fails most often in the field, and its warranty is usually much shorter than the panel's — typically 2–5 years for budget kits, up to 10–12 years for premium brands. If you've read our guide to [micro-inverter error codes](/blog/micro-inverter-error-codes-uk), you'll know these are also the part most likely to throw fault codes that look alarming but are often just a loose connector rather than a genuine failure.

**Cables, connectors and mounting hardware.** Often the weakest link warranty-wise — many kits cover these for just 1–2 years, on the assumption that a household will notice and report a damaged cable quickly. IP68 gland seals and MC4 connectors exposed to UK weather year-round are a realistic point of failure well before the panel itself degrades.

Read the box or listing carefully: a kit advertised as having a "25-year warranty" almost always means the panel's performance warranty, not a 25-year guarantee on the whole unit. The inverter and cabling underneath that headline figure are usually covered for a fraction of the time.

## What voids a plug-in solar warranty

Manufacturers write warranty exclusions with normal use in mind, but a surprising number of common DIY habits fall outside them:

- **Using an extension lead.** The [no-extension-lead rule](/blog/plug-in-solar-no-extension-lead-rule-uk) isn't just a legal requirement under the microgenerator spec — most manufacturers explicitly exclude damage caused by running the kit through anything other than the supplied cable plugged directly into a socket.
- **Non-standard mounting.** Drilling into a panel frame, using brackets not approved by the manufacturer, or mounting at an angle or location the instructions warn against (flush against a wall with no airflow, for example) can void coverage if it contributes to a fault.
- **Opening the micro-inverter housing.** Any attempt to open a sealed inverter unit — even just to inspect it after an error code — typically voids that component's warranty immediately, tamper-evident seals or not.
- **Exceeding the stated output configuration.** Wiring in an extra panel beyond what the kit specifies, or combining components from different manufacturers, is one of the fastest ways to void a warranty and, depending on the setup, breach the [one-unit-per-household rule](/blog/plug-in-solar-buying-checklist-uk) as well.
- **Second-hand kits.** Warranties are typically tied to the original purchaser and a registered proof of purchase. If you're considering [buying a second-hand system](/blog/buying-second-hand-solar-panels-uk), check whether the warranty transfers at all — many don't.

## Registering your warranty (don't skip this)

Most manufacturers require online registration within a set window — often 30 to 90 days of purchase — to activate the full warranty term. Miss it and you may be left with only a shorter statutory minimum. This is a five-minute job that's easy to forget in the excitement of getting a new kit [mounted and switched on](/blog/plug-in-solar-installation-cost-breakdown-uk), so do it the same day you unbox the panel, not "at some point."

Keep hold of:
- The original receipt or order confirmation
- Serial numbers for the panel and inverter separately
- Photos of the installed system, taken on day one, showing mounting and cable routing

That last point matters more than it sounds. If a claim ever comes down to a dispute about whether the installation itself caused a fault, day-one photos showing a compliant, manufacturer-approved setup are the strongest evidence you'll have.

## Statutory rights on top of the manufacturer's warranty

The manufacturer's warranty sits alongside your statutory rights under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, which apply regardless of what the box says. Goods bought in the UK must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described — and for the first six months after purchase, if something goes wrong, the fault is presumed to have existed at the time of sale unless the retailer can prove otherwise. That's a route back to the retailer (B&Q, Currys, Screwfix, Wickes, Lidl or Amazon) rather than the manufacturer, and it can be quicker than a manufacturer claims process, particularly for a kit that turns out to be [non-compliant with the microgenerator spec](/blog/how-to-spot-non-compliant-plug-in-solar-kit-uk) from the outset.

This is separate from, and doesn't replace, any home insurance implications — worth a read of our guide on [plug-in solar and home insurance](/blog/plug-in-solar-and-home-insurance-uk) and [fire risk cover](/blog/plug-in-solar-fire-risk-insurance-uk) if you haven't already told your insurer about the new system.

## What to check before you buy, not after

Given how many people are about to buy in the same narrow window around 27 August, it's worth spending five minutes on warranty terms before adding a kit to your basket, not after something goes wrong:

- How long is the panel's *product* warranty (defects), separate from its performance warranty (output degradation)?
- How long is the inverter covered — this is usually the real number that matters?
- Does the warranty require online registration, and within what window?
- Is UK-based support included, or do claims route through an overseas parent company?
- Does the warranty transfer if you [move house](/blog/moving-house-with-plug-in-solar-uk) and take the kit with you?

### How long is a typical plug-in solar panel warranty in the UK?

Most panels carry a product warranty of 10–12 years against manufacturing defects, and a separate performance warranty of up to 25 years guaranteeing a minimum output level. Micro-inverters are usually covered for a much shorter period, often 2–10 years depending on the brand.

### Does using an extension lead void my plug-in solar warranty?

Very likely, yes. Running a kit through anything other than the supplied cable, plugged directly into a socket, breaches both the legal no-extension-lead rule under the microgenerator spec and most manufacturers' warranty terms.

### What happens if my plug-in solar kit fails just outside the manufacturer's warranty?

You may still have a claim under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 against the retailer, particularly if the fault suggests the product wasn't of satisfactory quality when sold. This is separate from — and doesn't require — a manufacturer warranty claim.
