Nearly three weeks after it first appeared on the [ENA compliant devices registry](/blog/uk-compliant-plug-in-solar-devices-live), the Solarfy Solo 455 is still something of a mystery product — compliant on paper, but with no confirmed UK retailer, no published price, and no app or warranty information beyond the bare entry on the official database. That makes it a strange one to review, but also exactly the kind of kit buyers are searching for: it's new, it's small, and people want to know whether it's worth waiting for.

We covered the Solo 455 briefly in our [comparison against EcoFlow's STREAM range](/blog/solarfy-solo-455-vs-ecoflow-stream-uk) when it first landed on the register. This is a deeper, standalone look at what the listing actually tells you, what it doesn't, and who a 455W single-panel kit is realistically for.

## What's actually on the register

The ENA listing is sparse but specific. The system reference is `SOLAW/21640/V1`, filed by Solarfy Ltd, published on 25 September 2026, and classified simply as "Inverter" with a rated capacity of 0.455kW. It carries a passed G98 type test for grid connection — the legal minimum for a [plug-in microgenerator under SI 2026 No. 848](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk) — and there's no comments field listing a bundled panel, mounting hardware, or compatible third-party components, which most of the Worldwide Solar entries around it on the register do include.

That absence is worth reading correctly. It doesn't mean the kit is non-compliant or badly made; it means Solarfy either hasn't published the bundle details to the ENA, or is selling the inverter as a standalone unit for buyers to pair with their own panel. Either way, there's nothing here that tells you about build quality, app support, or what happens if it breaks in year three.

## Why 455W, specifically

Most of the plug-in kits UK buyers already know — EcoFlow's STREAM range, the various Hoymiles and APsystems-based bundles — sit at 600–800W, close to the [800VA/3.5A regulatory ceiling](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk). A 455W inverter is a noticeably more conservative design, and it has a couple of practical advantages that are easy to overlook if you're only comparing annual generation figures.

First, a single 400–450W panel is physically smaller and lighter than the twin-panel arrays most 800W kits ship with, which matters on a narrow balcony rail or a small fence panel where a dual-panel frame simply won't fit. Second, running comfortably under the regulatory cap gives a bit of headroom against any future tightening of the rules, and keeps the system firmly in "obviously fine" territory when your DNO reviews your [G98 notification](/blog/g98-dno-notification-step-by-step).

The trade-off is generation. Where an 800W STREAM-class kit might produce 500–650 kWh a year on a reasonable south-facing install, a 455W single-panel system is realistically looking at 280–350 kWh — roughly £70–£85 a year at Ofgem's current price cap, versus £120–£160 for the larger kits. That's a real gap, and it's the main thing to weigh against the Solo 455's smaller footprint.

## What's still missing

As of today, there's no UK retailer stocking the Solo 455, no published RRP, and no monitoring app named anywhere in the public listing. Compare that to the established players: EcoFlow's STREAM ships with a full app, a 10-year inverter warranty, and stock at B&Q, Currys and Amazon UK; Hoymiles and APsystems units have years of global deployment data behind them. Solarfy has none of that visible yet.

This isn't a criticism of the hardware itself — plenty of now-trusted brands started with a single ENA listing and no retail presence — but it does mean anyone buying a Solo 455 today would be buying ahead of the evidence. Our guide on [spotting a non-compliant plug-in solar kit](/blog/how-to-spot-non-compliant-plug-in-solar-kit-uk) is worth reading regardless of which kit you choose, since the register occasionally lists a hardware revision that differs slightly from what a seller ends up shipping.

## Installing it, if you get hold of one

Nothing about the Solo 455 changes the underlying installation rules. Any plug-in microgenerator sold in the UK has to connect through a standard BS 1363 plug, run through a 5A fuse, carry no battery wired into the plug circuit, and connect directly to the wall socket with no extension lead in between. You'll still need to [notify your DNO](/blog/g98-dno-notification-step-by-step) before switching it on, which for a sub-500W single-panel system is typically the simplest possible case — smaller inverters tend to sail through DNO review with the least friction of any category on the register.

## Who should wait, and who shouldn't bother

If your main constraint is physical space — a narrow Juliet balcony, a small fence panel, a spot where a twin-panel 800W frame genuinely won't fit — the Solo 455's smaller single-panel footprint is a good reason to keep half an eye on it as it (hopefully) reaches UK retail. If you want a kit you can actually buy, install this weekend, and monitor through a working app, the established options covered in our [best plug-in solar kit guide](/blog/best-plug-in-solar-kit-uk) remain the sensible default, and our roundup of [which UK retailers stock plug-in solar](/blog/retailers-stocking-plug-in-solar-uk-2026) covers where to find them.

It's also worth checking our running list of [newly ENA-approved kits](/blog/new-ena-approved-plug-in-solar-kits-october-2026-uk) periodically — Solarfy is unlikely to be the last small manufacturer to list a conservative, single-panel inverter aimed at the gap below the 800W mainstream.

## Verdict

The Solarfy Solo 455 is a legitimate, G98-compliant plug-in microgenerator on paper, and a 455W single-panel design fills a real niche for space-constrained balconies and fences. But "compliant" and "buyable" aren't the same thing yet — there's no price, no retailer, no app, and no warranty published. Treat it as one to watch rather than one to order, and check back on the [live registry](/blog/uk-compliant-plug-in-solar-devices-live) and our comparison piece for updates once retail details land.

### Can I buy the Solarfy Solo 455 in the UK right now?

Not through any confirmed retailer as of this review. It appears on the official ENA compliance register with a passed G98 test, but no price, stockist, or app has been published yet.

### Is a 455W plug-in solar kit worth it compared to an 800W kit?

It depends on space and budget. A 455W single-panel kit generates less annually (roughly 280–350 kWh versus 500–650 kWh for an 800W kit) but suits narrow balconies and smaller mounting spots where a twin-panel frame won't fit.

### How do I check if a new plug-in solar kit is genuinely ENA-compliant?

Search the manufacturer name and model against our [live ENA registry page](/blog/uk-compliant-plug-in-solar-devices-live), which syncs from the official Energy Networks Association database. Match the exact system reference the seller advertises against the listing before buying.
