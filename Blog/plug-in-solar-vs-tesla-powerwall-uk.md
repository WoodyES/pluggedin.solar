Since plug-in solar became legal under [SI 2026 No. 848](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk) on 27 August 2026, a lot of UK homeowners researching home energy have found themselves comparing two very different products: a £699 plug-in solar kit from [EcoFlow](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=51797&awinaffid=2846734&ued=https%3A%2F%2Fuk.ecoflow.com%2Fproducts%2Fstream-garden-ground-kit) and a £6,000-plus Tesla Powerwall whole-home battery. They get lumped together because both involve "solar and batteries," but they solve almost entirely different problems, for almost entirely different households.

This guide breaks down what each actually does, what each costs, and — most usefully — when you might eventually want both.

## They're not actually competitors

A Tesla Powerwall is a 13.5kWh stationary home battery, installed by a certified electrician, that connects into your consumer unit and typically pairs with a full rooftop solar array (4kW or more) fitted under MCS certification. It's designed to store surplus daytime generation and discharge it in the evening, and it can provide whole-home backup during a power cut if configured with Tesla's Backup Gateway.

A [plug-in solar kit](/blog/plug-in-solar-uk-complete-guide) is a legally capped 800VA/3.5A microgeneration system — one or two panels, a microinverter, and a standard BS 1363 plug into a normal wall socket. It cannot power your whole home, cannot legally include a battery wired into the same circuit under the plug-in solar rules, and generates at most around 800W at peak — enough to offset a fridge, a few lights, and background electronics, not your whole consumption.

In short: Powerwall is a backup-and-storage system that usually complements a full rooftop solar installation. Plug-in solar is a low-commitment way to shave a modest amount off your electricity bill without scaffolding, planning permission, or an MCS-certified installer.

## Cost comparison

| | Plug-in solar kit | Tesla Powerwall (with rooftop solar) |
|---|---|---|
| Typical cost | £400–£900 | £6,000–£12,000+ installed |
| Installation | DIY, plug into a socket | Certified electrician, DNO notification required |
| Annual saving | £80–£180 | £400–£900+ (with time-of-use tariff optimisation) |
| Payback period | 3–6 years | 8–15 years |
| Power cut backup | No | Yes (with Backup Gateway) |
| Planning/DNO process | Simple [G98 notification](/blog/g98-dno-notification-step-by-step) | Full MCS + DNO application |

The Powerwall's payback period is longer in absolute terms, but it's solving a different problem — resilience and large-scale self-consumption — not just a marginal bill reduction. If you already have rooftop solar generating more than you use, a battery captures value that would otherwise be exported at a low [SEG rate](/blog/plug-in-solar-export-payments-seg-uk). Plug-in solar has nothing to capture in that scenario because it's not meant to scale that high.

## Who should buy plug-in solar

- Renters and leaseholders who can't install rooftop solar at all — see our guide to [plug-in solar for renters](/blog/plug-in-solar-for-renters-uk)

- Flat and balcony dwellers — [balcony solar](/blog/balcony-solar-uk-guide) is explicitly what the new rules were designed to unlock
- Anyone who wants to try solar without an electrician, DNO paperwork complexity, or five-figure spend

## Who should buy a Tesla Powerwall (with rooftop solar)

- Homeowners with south-facing roof space suited to a 4kW+ array and the budget for a full MCS-certified install
- Households wanting backup power during outages, particularly in areas with frequent interruptions
- Anyone on a time-of-use import tariff who wants to shift grid charging to cheap overnight rates and discharge during expensive peak hours
- Households already exporting significant surplus solar at a low SEG rate who'd rather store and self-consume it

If this is you, the economics depend heavily on your roof orientation, existing consumption pattern, and tariff — a proper MCS quote with modelled generation data is essential before committing.

## Can you have both?

Yes, and increasingly people do, just not on the same circuit. A household might have a full rooftop array feeding a Powerwall for the main home, plus a separate plug-in solar kit in the garden or on a garage roof purely for outbuilding use — a shed, greenhouse, or garden office. These are entirely independent systems because the [plug-in solar rules explicitly prohibit wiring a battery into the plug-in circuit](/blog/plug-in-solar-no-battery-rule-uk-explained); any battery pairing has to happen on the standalone, portable side (an [EcoFlow DELTA-style power station](/blog/best-portable-power-station-plug-in-solar-uk) charged separately), not integrated into the mains-connected microgeneration circuit.

For most first-time buyers, though, the sensible order is: try plug-in solar first, see how much you actually save and how much you engage with monitoring your generation, then decide whether a five-figure rooftop-and-battery investment is worth it. Our [plug-in solar vs rooftop solar comparison](/blog/plug-in-solar-vs-rooftop-solar-uk-2026) goes deeper on that decision if you're still weighing it up, and the [current ENA-compliant device list](/blog/uk-compliant-plug-in-solar-devices-live) is the place to check before buying any plug-in kit.

## FAQ

### Can a Tesla Powerwall be installed without rooftop solar?

Technically yes — it can charge from the grid on a time-of-use tariff and discharge during peak hours — but most installers and most of the economic case assume it's paired with solar generation. Without solar, payback stretches considerably and depends almost entirely on the spread between your cheap and expensive tariff rates.

### Is plug-in solar a stepping stone to a full Powerwall setup?

For many households, yes. It's a low-cost way to get comfortable with solar generation, monitoring apps, and self-consumption habits before committing to a full MCS-certified installation. The two systems don't share hardware, so there's no wasted spend — the plug-in kit keeps working in the garden or on a shed even after a full rooftop system goes in.

### Why can't I just add a battery to my plug-in solar kit?

UK regulations for plug-in microgenerators explicitly prohibit a battery wired into the same circuit as the mains-connected plug-in panel — see our [explainer on the no-battery rule](/blog/plug-in-solar-no-battery-rule-uk-explained). You can still use a separate, standalone portable power station charged independently, but it isn't integrated into the plug-in circuit itself.
