Daylight Saving Time ends at 2 a.m. on Sunday, November 1, 2026, across every US state that observes it (sorry, Arizona and Hawaii — this one doesn't apply to you). Every year this trips up the same misconception: that the clock change itself somehow reduces solar generation. It doesn't. What actually changes is when the sun is up relative to your clock, and that has real consequences for how you should be using a plug-in solar + battery setup even though total daylight hours are unaffected by the switch itself.

## What the clock change does and doesn't do

The clocks moving back an hour doesn't change sunrise or sunset times in absolute terms — the sun still rises and sets exactly when astronomy says it will. What changes is the clock reading attached to those events. On October 31, sunset might read around 6:00 p.m. local time; on November 1, the same physical sunset now reads 5:00 p.m. Your panels generate exactly the same total daily output either day — the sun doesn't know what day it is. What shifts is your daily routine relative to daylight.

This matters because most of the real seasonal drop in solar output through November and December comes from genuinely shorter days and a lower sun angle, not from the clock change. For the mechanics of that broader seasonal decline, see our [net metering vs. net billing explainer](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) for how utilities handle the lower generation months differently depending on your state's compensation structure.

## Why this actually matters for plug-in solar owners

A plug-in kit feeding a portable power station, rather than exporting to the grid, lives and dies by when you charge versus when you draw down. The clock change moves your evening routine — dinner, lights on, device charging — to an hour earlier relative to the sun, straight into the window when your panels have already stopped producing for the day. If you've been timing power station charging around "charge during the day, draw down in the evening," that evening draw-down now starts an hour earlier on the clock, while your panels still finish at the same solar time they always did.

Practically: if your battery was comfortably covering a 5–9 p.m. evening draw through October, that's now functionally a 4–8 p.m. draw by the clock, landing closer to when your panels are already in their late-afternoon output taper. The fix isn't more equipment — it's just recalibrating when you expect the battery to start carrying the load.

## Three things worth doing this week

- **Recheck your power station's charge schedule** if it has app-based scheduling (EcoFlow, Anker SOLIX and Jackery apps all support this) — shift any time-based charging windows to match the new clock, not the old one.
- **Notice your utility's time-of-use windows**, if you're on one. Many utilities define peak pricing by clock time, not solar time, so a peak window that started as the sun was setting in September might now start while the sun is still up in November — a small win if you can route some draw to the afternoon instead.
- **Re-baseline your expectations for "normal" generation.** A reading that looked low in September at 5 p.m. might be completely normal for November at 4 p.m. — don't assume a fault just because the app's hourly output chart looks different than it did two months ago. If output genuinely drops more than the season explains, see our [troubleshooting guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-not-working-troubleshooting-us) before assuming it's a DST-related issue, because it won't be.

## States where this barely matters

Arizona and Hawaii don't observe Daylight Saving Time at all, so nothing changes for those installations beyond the natural seasonal daylight curve. If you're in either state, skip straight to seasonal planning — see our [Arizona](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-arizona) or [Hawaii 2026 guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-hawaii-2026-guide) for state-specific context instead.

## Looking ahead to winter proper

DST ending is really just the marker that winter solar planning season has started. Shorter days, a lower sun angle, and more cloud cover through December and January will do far more to your generation totals than this one clock change ever could. If you haven't already, now's the time to review panel angle and placement before the sun gets any lower — our [where to put plug-in solar panels](/us/blog/where-to-put-plug-in-solar-panels-us) guide covers the adjustments worth making before the solstice, and if you're in a storm-prone state, pair that with the [storm-proofing guide](/us/blog/storm-proofing-plug-in-solar-us) while you're out there anyway.

### Does the DST clock change actually reduce my total solar generation?

No. Total daylight hours and sun angle are unaffected by the clock change itself — your panels generate the same regardless of what the clock says. Only the clock-time label on sunrise and sunset shifts.

### Should I adjust my power station's charging schedule for DST?

Yes, if you use app-based time scheduling. Shift any clock-time-based charge or discharge windows by the same hour the clocks moved, so they still align with actual daylight rather than the old schedule.

### Why does my solar app show lower output right after the clock change?

Almost always normal seasonal decline (shorter days, lower sun angle) coinciding with the clock change, not caused by it. Compare hourly generation curves by solar time rather than clock time to see this clearly — the curve shape should look similar, just shifted an hour earlier on your phone's clock.
