Tennessee is an unusual state for home solar because almost the entire state buys electricity through the Tennessee Valley Authority system rather than a typical investor-owned utility. TVA sets the rules its local power companies follow, there's no state net metering law, and Tennessee has no state income tax to offer a credit against in the first place. For a small plug-in solar kit, though, most of that is beside the point — here's what actually matters if you're in Nashville, Memphis, Knoxville, Chattanooga, or anywhere served by a TVA local power company.

## Where plug-in solar sits legally in Tennessee

Like the rest of the country, small plug-in solar kits occupy a legal grey area rather than a clearly regulated category. There's no Tennessee statute that specifically addresses a 200–800W panel plugged into a standard wall outlet, and the state's utility regulators focus their rules on larger, formally interconnected systems. In practice, that means the framework that matters is [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us), the National Electrical Code section covering interconnected power sources, plus whatever your inverter's own certification covers.

Most Tennessee households running a sub-800W kit with a [UL 1741-listed microinverter](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) do so without going through formal utility interconnection paperwork at all — the system is small enough, and designed to self-consume rather than export, that it doesn't trigger the net-metering registration processes built for rooftop arrays. If you're unsure whether your specific local power company (there are more than 20 TVA distributors across the state, from Nashville Electric Service to Memphis Light, Gas and Water) has a different stance, a quick call to their interconnection desk is worth the ten minutes. Our [state-by-state legality guide](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-in-my-state) has more on how this plays out elsewhere.

## No net metering law changes the math

This is the part that trips up people moving to Tennessee from a state like California or New York. TVA's local power companies are not required to offer net metering, and most that do offer it provide significantly less favorable terms than a true 1:1 credit — some use a flat buyback rate well below the retail price. Our [net metering vs. net billing guide](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) explains the mechanics, but the short version for Tennessee is: don't buy a plug-in kit expecting to sell power back to the grid at retail rates, because in most of the state you simply can't.

What this actually means in practice is that a small plug-in kit is arguably a better fit for Tennessee than a large rooftop array would be. Since these kits are sized to offset a household's own daytime baseline load — a refrigerator, a modem, some lighting — rather than to export surplus, the lack of favorable net metering barely affects the economics. You're not trying to sell power back; you're just not buying as much of it in the first place. Retail electricity rates across TVA territory run roughly 11–13 cents per kWh, on the lower end nationally, which does mean payback periods run a little longer than in a high-rate state — our [payback period calculator guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-payback-period-us) walks through the math for your specific rate.

## What to actually buy and where to put it

For most Tennessee homes, an 800W kit with a UL 1741-listed microinverter is the practical ceiling for a plug-and-play setup — see our [buying checklist](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-buying-checklist-us) for what to look for before you order. Middle Tennessee's hot, humid summers mean good airflow around the inverter matters for longevity, and if you're in a storm-prone part of the state — Tennessee sees its share of severe spring weather, including tornado risk across the Cumberland Plateau and Middle Tennessee — it's worth reading the [storm-proofing guide](/us/blog/storm-proofing-plug-in-solar-us) before you commit to a mounting setup. A removable ground-mount frame that can be brought in ahead of a severe weather warning is a genuinely useful feature here, not just a nice-to-have.

South-facing orientation matters more in Tennessee than in far-southern states, since the sun angle is lower for more of the year — a wall or ground frame with some tilt adjustment will outperform a flat-mounted panel meaningfully across fall and winter. If your setup isn't producing what you expected, our [troubleshooting guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-not-working-troubleshooting-us) covers the most common causes, from shading to loose connections.

Installation itself is genuinely simple — no electrician, no permit, no roof penetration — but it's worth reading through our [installation guide](/us/blog/how-to-install-plug-in-solar-us) and [electrical safety basics](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-electrical-safety-120v) once before you start, since a dedicated outdoor-rated outlet and a GFCI-protected circuit are both worth confirming rather than assuming.

### Does Tennessee have a state solar tax credit?

No. Tennessee has no state income tax, so there's no state tax credit to offer against solar purchases the way states like South Carolina do. The federal residential clean energy framework may still apply depending on system type and your specific setup — check current federal guidance, since a small plug-in kit's eligibility can differ from a full rooftop installation's.

### Can I sell excess power back to the grid in Tennessee?

Generally not at a meaningful rate. Most TVA local power companies either don't offer net metering or offer a buyback rate well below the retail price you pay. This is one more reason a modest, self-consumption-sized plug-in kit makes more sense here than an oversized system aimed at exporting power.

### Which Tennessee utility do I need to check with before buying?

It depends on your address — Nashville Electric Service, Memphis Light, Gas and Water, Knoxville Utilities Board, EPB in Chattanooga, and roughly 20 other TVA local power companies each set their own interconnection stance. For a sub-800W self-consumption kit, most households don't need to file anything, but a quick call confirms your specific provider's current position.
