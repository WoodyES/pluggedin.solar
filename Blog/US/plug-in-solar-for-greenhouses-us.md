Backyard greenhouse owners run into the same problem every fall: the greenhouse is often the farthest outlet from the house, the loads are small but constant, and running a new circuit out to it is expensive if you're not already planning other electrical work. A plug-in solar kit — a small panel array paired with a [UL 1741-listed microinverter](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) that feeds into a standard 120V outlet — is a genuinely useful fit for this specific problem, as long as you're realistic about what it can and can't power.

This guide covers what a 200-800W plug-in kit can run in a typical backyard greenhouse, where it falls short, and how to think about the [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) gray area that governs this entire product category in the US.

## What a greenhouse actually needs power for

Most backyard greenhouses have a fairly predictable load profile:

- **Automatic vent openers** — low-wattage actuators, often under 10W, that open roof vents when internal temperature rises
- **Circulation fans** — typically 20-50W, run more or less continuously during the growing season
- **Irrigation pumps** — intermittent, usually 50-150W when running
- **Heat mats and propagator warming cables** — these are the heavy hitters, often 100-300W per mat and frequently run for hours at a stretch in cooler months
- **Supplemental grow lighting** — highly variable, from a few LED strips at under 50W to full-spectrum panels pulling several hundred watts

Add it up and a greenhouse with vents, a fan, intermittent irrigation and one or two heat mats is commonly sitting in the 150-400W continuous-ish range during the shoulder seasons when you need it most — which lines up reasonably well with what an 800W plug-in kit can supply during daylight hours.

## Sizing the kit to the season

The catch is timing: a greenhouse's heaviest loads (heat mats, frost protection) peak exactly when solar generation is weakest — late fall through early spring, with short days and low sun angles. A plug-in kit is still useful here, but as *generation that reduces grid draw* rather than *generation that fully covers the load*. During summer, when heat mats are off and loads drop to just fans and vent motors, the same kit will often cover close to 100% of greenhouse consumption on a clear day.

If you're sizing specifically for this use case, our [sizing calculator](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-sizing-calculator-us) is worth running with your actual greenhouse wattage rather than a generic household estimate — greenhouse loads are smaller and steadier than most household appliances, which changes the self-consumption math in your favor even with a modest kit.

## Wiring it in without overcomplicating things

The practical setup is the same one that applies to any other [garage or workshop installation](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-for-garage-workshop-us): the microinverter's output goes into a standard outlet on the same circuit as the greenhouse's existing loads, the panels sit on a south-facing rack near the structure (roof-mounted on the greenhouse itself if it's sturdy enough, ground- or fence-mounted otherwise), and nothing about the setup involves battery storage or backfeeding beyond what the inverter's built-in safety disconnect allows for.

A few things worth getting right specifically for a greenhouse installation:

- **Keep panels clear of the greenhouse's own shading** — tall plants, shade cloth and the greenhouse structure itself can all throw shadows onto a roof-mounted panel array depending on time of day
- **Route cabling away from irrigation and condensation** — greenhouses are humid environments by design, so a [weatherproof, outdoor-rated connection](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-cable-accessories-us) matters more here than almost anywhere else a plug-in kit gets installed
- **Don't daisy-chain extension cords to reach a far corner** — if the greenhouse outlet is a genuine stretch from where panels can usefully sit, that's a sign to reconsider the layout rather than add cable length

## The HOA and permitting angle

If your greenhouse sits in a backyard governed by an HOA, the same considerations apply as to any other [small plug-in solar installation under HOA rules](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-and-your-hoa) — many HOAs that restrict visible rooftop solar arrays have no equivalent rule for small portable or ground-mounted panels tucked beside an accessory structure, but it's worth checking your specific covenant rather than assuming. Utility interconnection requirements vary by state and provider too, so if your greenhouse setup pushes your household's total plug-in solar capacity close to or above the 800W-1200W range some utilities use as an informal notification threshold, it's worth reviewing your [state's specific rules](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-in-my-state) before adding a second kit.

## Is it worth it compared to running a new circuit?

For most backyard greenhouses, yes. A 400-800W plug-in kit costs a fraction of trenching and running a dedicated circuit from the main panel, installs in an afternoon, and directly offsets the kind of small, steady load a greenhouse produces. It won't replace grid power during a week of heavy cloud in December when your heat mats are working hardest, but it meaningfully cuts the electric bill line item most greenhouse owners don't think about until they see it — and it pays for itself faster than almost any other application of this product category precisely because the load profile is so steady and predictable.

### Can a plug-in solar kit power a greenhouse heat mat overnight?

No — without battery storage, a plug-in kit only generates power while the sun is up, so overnight heat mat use still draws entirely from the grid. The kit offsets daytime heating and other loads, reducing your net electric bill rather than eliminating it.

### Do I need an electrician to add solar to my greenhouse circuit?

For a standard plug-in kit feeding into an existing outlet via its own UL 1741-listed microinverter, most owners install it themselves, the same way they would any other 120V appliance. Check your local utility's interconnection rules and any HOA covenant first, since requirements vary by state.

### Will panel output drop in a humid greenhouse environment?

The panels themselves typically sit outside or on the greenhouse roof exterior, so ambient humidity inside the structure doesn't affect their output. What matters more is keeping the microinverter and connectors weatherproofed against the condensation and irrigation overspray common around greenhouses.
