Connecticut residents pay some of the highest electricity rates in the continental United States — often 30-38 cents/kWh once delivery charges are included, well above the national average. That rate environment makes small-scale plug-in solar unusually attractive on a pure payback-per-dollar basis, even though the state's two major utilities have specific interconnection processes you need to understand before you plug anything in.

## Why Connecticut's rates matter for plug-in solar

Plug-in solar kits (typically 200-800W with a UL 1741-listed microinverter) are a grey area under [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) — not explicitly banned, but not addressed by name either, since the code was written assuming licensed, permitted interconnections. What that means practically is that adoption is happening informally, kit by kit, ahead of clear state guidance.

At Connecticut's retail rates, a single 800W kit generating roughly 900-1,000 kWh/year offsets $270-$380 annually — noticeably more than the same system would save in a low-rate state like Washington or Idaho. That's the core appeal: Connecticut's high rates compress payback periods even on a small system. See our general [plug-in solar payback breakdown](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-payback-period-us) for the underlying math.

## Eversource and United Illuminating

Connecticut is served almost entirely by two investor-owned utilities:

- **Eversource** — covers most of the state, including Hartford and the northern and western regions
- **United Illuminating (UI)** — covers the New Haven and Bridgeport areas in the south

Both utilities operate net metering programs for permitted, licensed solar interconnections, but neither has a formal small-kit or "plug-and-play" interconnection tier — Connecticut hasn't followed Utah, Germany, or the UK's newly created plug-in microgenerator category. For a sub-800W kit plugged into an ordinary household outlet, you're operating in the same informal space as most of the US: outside the interconnection agreement process entirely, exporting a small, largely self-consumed amount rather than being metered as a generation source.

This matters for how you should think about the system: treat it as **load-reduction equipment**, not an interconnected generation asset. See our explainer on [net metering vs net billing](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) for how formal interconnected solar differs from what a plug-in kit is actually doing on your circuit.

## Sizing for Connecticut's climate

Connecticut sits around 41-42°N, giving it moderate solar irradiance — less than the Sun Belt states, comparable to much of the UK and northern Europe. Expect:

| Season | Typical daily generation (800W kit) |
|---|---|
| Summer (Jun-Aug) | 3.0-3.8 kWh/day |
| Spring/Fall | 2.0-2.8 kWh/day |
| Winter (Dec-Feb) | 0.8-1.5 kWh/day |

Snow cover is a real factor in a Connecticut winter — panels mounted at a steep angle (35-45°) shed snow faster than flatter balcony or ground mounts. Nor'easters bring both high winds and heavy wet snow in the same storm, so secure mounting hardware matters more here than in milder climates; our [storm-proofing guide](/us/blog/storm-proofing-plug-in-solar-us) covers wind-rated mounting even though it was written with hurricane country in mind — the same principles apply.

## HOA and condo considerations

Connecticut has a high proportion of condo and HOA-governed housing, especially in Fairfield County and along the shoreline. Connecticut's solar-access statutes primarily protect *permitted, roof-mounted* systems from unreasonable HOA restriction — a balcony-clipped or ground-set plug-in kit sits in a less protected grey area. If you're in an HOA community, read the by-laws on exterior modifications before installing, and see our [HOA rules for plug-in solar](/us/blog/hoa-rules-plug-in-solar-us) guide for how to approach that conversation.

## Federal incentives still apply

Connecticut doesn't run a state-specific rebate program for small plug-in kits, but the federal residential clean energy framework can apply to qualifying solar equipment purchases — see our [federal tax credit guide](/us/blog/federal-tax-credit-plug-in-solar) for eligibility details, since rules and qualifying equipment lists change and are worth checking against your specific purchase.

## Is it worth it in Connecticut?

Given the state's above-average electricity rates, a modest plug-in solar setup pays for itself faster here than in most of the country — often inside 2-3 years for a $600-900 kit, even accounting for Connecticut's more modest solar resource compared to sunnier states. The main watch-items are winter snow load on mounting hardware and confirming your specific building or HOA's rules before installing anything visible from the street. For the general framework on whether a kit makes sense for your household, our [is plug-in solar worth it](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-worth-it-us) guide covers the broader decision factors, and [best plug-in solar kits](/us/blog/best-plug-in-solar-kits-us) rounds up current options.

### Does Connecticut have a specific interconnection process for plug-in solar kits?

No. Eversource and United Illuminating both run standard net metering programs for licensed, permitted solar installs, but neither has created a lightweight tier for small plug-in kits. Most owners treat these systems as self-consumption load-reduction equipment rather than formally interconnected generation.

### How much can a plug-in solar kit save in Connecticut?

At typical Connecticut retail rates of 30-38c/kWh, an 800W kit generating roughly 900-1,000 kWh/year of usable self-consumption can offset $270-$380 annually, giving a payback period around 2-3 years on a $600-900 kit — faster than in most lower-rate states.

### Do I need a permit to plug in a small solar kit in Connecticut?

Requirements vary by municipality, and Connecticut has not issued statewide guidance specifically for sub-800W plug-in kits. Check with your local building department and your utility before installing, and always use a UL 1741-listed inverter regardless of permitting status.
