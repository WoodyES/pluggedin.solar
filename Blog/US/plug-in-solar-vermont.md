Vermont is an unusual case for plug-in solar: a state with genuinely strong, pro-solar policy and net metering rules, paired with a solar resource that's modest by national standards and winters that cut deeply into daily generation for nearly half the year. Buyers who understand both halves of that picture tend to be happiest with the results.

## Vermont's Solar Resource

Vermont gets around 3.8-4.2 peak sun hours per day on average across the year — lower than most of the country, closer to Washington State or Michigan than to its Northeast neighbors like Massachusetts. The state's heavily wooded terrain and frequent cloud cover, especially in the Champlain Valley and the Green Mountains, bring that average down further at specific sites. Summer is genuinely strong, with long days and clear stretches; it's December through February where output drops off sharply.

A 400-600W plug-in kit in a reasonably unshaded Vermont location typically generates **550-750 kWh per year**, skewed heavily toward the April-September window. At Vermont's average residential electricity rate of roughly $0.21-0.23/kWh — among the higher rates in the country, driven partly by the state's heating-dominated grid — that works out to $115-170 in annual savings, giving a payback in the 4-6 year range on a typical kit, better than many sunnier but cheaper-electricity states. For the general math behind these numbers, see our [plug-in solar payback period](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-payback-period-us) guide.

## The Legal and Code Picture

Vermont has no dedicated plug-in solar statute, so small kits fall under the same general framework as most of the country: [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) governs interconnected power production equipment, and UL 1741-listed microinverters are built to operate safely within it. Vermont's Public Utility Commission (PUC) oversees interconnection standards statewide, and the state is generally regarded as having one of the more structured, well-documented interconnection processes in New England. See our [state-by-state legal guide](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-in-my-state) for how Vermont compares nationally.

## Net Metering: Vermont's Real Advantage

This is where Vermont stands out. The state has maintained a genuine net metering program — not the net billing structures now common in states like California — meaning exported solar generation is credited close to retail rate rather than at a steep discount. Vermont also permits **group net metering**, letting multiple customers share credits from a single system, a structure more commonly associated with community solar than small residential kits but worth knowing about if you're part of a multi-unit property or housing cooperative. Our [net metering vs net billing](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) guide explains why this distinction matters more for larger systems than for a single plug-in kit, where self-consumption still does most of the work.

## Utility Territory: Green Mountain Power and the Co-ops

Green Mountain Power (GMP) serves roughly 70% of Vermont and has one of the more progressive interconnection and storage-incentive postures of any utility in the country, including programs that pay customers to let GMP tap into home battery storage during peak demand. The rest of the state is served by a patchwork of municipal utilities and co-ops, including Vermont Electric Cooperative and Burlington Electric. Processes and response times vary by provider; our [utility interconnection guide](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) covers the general contact and notification steps regardless of which one serves your address.

## Winter Is the Defining Constraint

Vermont's short winter days and frequent snow cover are the biggest practical factor, more than for almost any other state in this guide.

- **Steep sun angle.** At roughly 43-45°N latitude, Vermont's winter sun path is low and short. A kit mounted flat for summer performance will badly underperform from November through February; an adjustable or steeply angled mount recovers much of that loss.
- **Snow accumulation.** Vermont gets substantial snowfall most winters. A panel buried under even a few inches of snow generates close to nothing — ground and rail-mounted kits need to be accessible enough to clear after storms, which our [storm-proofing guide](/us/blog/storm-proofing-plug-in-solar-us) touches on more generally.
- **Shoulder-season value.** Vermont's maple-sugaring season and shoulder months (March-April, October) often bring clear, cold, high-albedo days where snow reflection can briefly boost output above nameplate — a quirk worth knowing rather than relying on for sizing.

## HOA and Deed Restrictions

Vermont doesn't have a strong statewide solar-access law overriding private covenants, so restrictions can still apply in some planned communities, though they're less common than in denser suburban states. Our [HOA rules guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-and-your-hoa) covers what's typically negotiable, and it's worth checking any deed restriction before buying if you're in one of Vermont's newer planned developments.

## Bottom Line

Vermont's solar resource is modest and its winters genuinely cut into output, but strong net metering and relatively high electricity rates mean the financial case still works out reasonably well — better, in fact, than several sunnier states with cheap power. Get the mount angle right for winter, plan for snow clearing, and a plug-in kit will do solid work across a Vermont summer and shoulder season.

### Is plug-in solar legal in Vermont?

There's no dedicated state statute specifically addressing plug-in solar kits. Small UL 1741-listed systems operate under the general NEC Article 705 framework used nationally, with Vermont's PUC overseeing interconnection standards.

### Does Vermont still offer true net metering?

Yes. Vermont maintains genuine net metering, crediting exported generation close to retail rate, including a group net metering option for shared or multi-unit systems — a real advantage over states that have moved to lower-value net billing structures.

### How much does Vermont's winter affect a plug-in solar kit?

Significantly. Short days, a low sun angle, and snow cover mean output drops sharply from November through February. An adjustable, steeply angled mount and a habit of clearing snow after storms recover much of that seasonal loss.
