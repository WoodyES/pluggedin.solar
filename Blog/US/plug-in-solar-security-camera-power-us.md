Outdoor security cameras are one of the most common reasons people start looking at small solar setups in the first place, especially for garages, driveways, sheds, and detached structures where running new electrical wiring isn't practical or affordable. The good news is that this is one of the better matches for a small plug-in solar setup — but the details of how you wire it matter more than with almost any other use case, because a camera that drops offline defeats the entire point of having one.

## Why cameras are a good match — with one big caveat

A typical wired outdoor security camera draws somewhere between 5W and 15W continuously, and a PoE (Power over Ethernet) NVR system with multiple cameras might draw 30-60W total. That's a tiny load compared to what even a modest [400W plug-in solar kit](/us/blog/best-400w-solar-panel-us-2026) can generate on a sunny day, which is exactly why this pairing gets recommended so often.

The caveat is the same one that applies to every always-on load with a grid-tied plug-in setup: a basic microinverter-based kit has no battery, so it only generates power while the sun is up. If your camera is plugged directly into a wall outlet that's also fed by your plug-in solar kit, it runs fine all day and simply reverts to drawing from the grid at night — which is a completely normal and safe setup, since the camera was always going to need grid power at night anyway. The solar kit just reduces how much of that camera's draw comes from your utility bill during daylight hours.

## Two very different setups, don't confuse them

This is the point where a lot of buyers get confused, so it's worth being explicit: there are two completely different categories of "solar-powered security camera" setup, and they are not interchangeable.

**Grid-tied plug-in solar feeding a wired camera system** — a [UL 1741-listed microinverter kit](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) plugged into a standard outlet that also powers your existing wired cameras or NVR. This offsets your electricity draw during the day but provides no backup power if the grid goes down, since the inverter shuts off without a grid connection to synchronize to, per the rules in [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us).

**A standalone battery-and-panel system dedicated to the camera** — a small off-grid solar panel wired directly to a battery, which then powers the camera independently of your home's electrical system entirely. This is a completely different kind of setup, closer to what a lot of truly "solar security camera" products on the market already are out of the box, and it keeps running during a power outage because it never depended on grid power to begin with.

If your priority is keeping cameras running specifically during an outage — a real concern after a storm, when break-ins often spike — the off-grid battery route is the one that actually achieves that. A grid-tied plug-in kit is better suited to simply lowering your electricity bill for cameras you're already running day and night. Our [winter storm power outage prep guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-winter-storm-power-outage-prep-us) and [hurricane resilience guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-hurricane-resilience-us) both cover this distinction in more depth if backup power during severe weather is the actual goal.

## Sizing a kit for cameras alongside other loads

In practice, almost nobody buys a plug-in solar kit purely to power security cameras — the load is too small to justify it on its own. Cameras are usually one small piece of a broader daytime draw that a kit is offsetting, alongside things like a garage fridge, pool pump, or general household use. If cameras genuinely are your main motivation — say, for a detached garage or workshop with no other major daytime load — a single [400W panel kit](/us/blog/best-400w-solar-panel-us-2026) is more than sufficient; you don't need to size up to 800W or beyond for a load this light.

## Don't skip the electrical safety basics

Outdoor security camera power is often routed through weatherproof outlets or junction boxes already, which makes it tempting to treat the whole project casually. Don't. Confirm your microinverter is UL 1741-listed, keep the connection on a standard interior or GFCI-protected outdoor outlet rather than an improvised extension run, and check our [electrical safety guide for 120V plug-in setups](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-electrical-safety-120v) before wiring anything near a garage or shed that also handles other equipment. If you're renting rather than owning the property, see our [renters' guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-for-renters-us) first — a camera installation is exactly the kind of modification a landlord may want advance notice of.

Before buying any specific model, it's worth checking our [best plug-in solar kits](/us/blog/best-plug-in-solar-kits-us) roundup and our broader [is plug-in solar worth it](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-worth-it-us) overview — sizing this correctly for your actual daytime load (cameras plus whatever else is running) matters more than chasing the cheapest panel you can find.

### Will my security cameras stay online if the power goes out?

Not with a standard grid-tied plug-in solar kit — the microinverter shuts off without a grid connection to synchronize to, per NEC Article 705. If outage resilience for your cameras matters, you need a separate off-grid battery-and-panel setup dedicated to the camera, not a grid-tied kit.

### How big a solar kit do I need just for security cameras?

Very small. A single wired camera draws 5-15W; even a multi-camera NVR system rarely exceeds 60W. A 400W panel kit covers this with substantial headroom for other daytime loads on the same circuit.

### Can I plug a solar kit directly into my camera's power adapter?

No — a plug-in solar microinverter feeds into your home's standard electrical wiring through a wall outlet, not directly into a low-voltage camera adapter. The camera stays plugged into its normal power source; the solar kit simply offsets the electricity that outlet draws from the grid during the day.
