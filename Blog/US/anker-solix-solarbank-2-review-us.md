Anker's SOLIX Solarbank 2 built its reputation in Europe as a balcony-solar storage box — a battery with built-in MPPT solar charging and a microinverter that feeds a home circuit through a wall outlet. It's increasingly showing up in US shopping searches too, usually bought alongside a pair of portable panels rather than through any formal utility program. Before treating it as a plug-and-play alternative to a full rooftop system, it's worth understanding exactly what the Solarbank 2 is built to do and where US rules create friction that doesn't exist in the markets it was originally designed for.

## What the Solarbank 2 actually is

At its core, the Solarbank 2 is a stackable battery (commonly sold in 1.6-2.5kWh capacities, with some variants expandable further) paired with a built-in solar MPPT controller and a grid-tied microinverter output, typically capped around 800W. You connect one or two solar panels to the unit's DC input, and it manages charging the battery, then releasing stored power into your home's circuit through its AC output — either on a schedule or dynamically, depending on the app settings. That's a meaningfully different design to the battery-less kits covered in our [best plug-in solar kits](/us/blog/best-plug-in-solar-kits-us) roundup, and closer in concept to [EcoFlow's PowerStream](/us/blog/ecoflow-powerstream-review-us) — both products are built around storing solar generation first and exporting any genuine surplus second.

## Compliance: the same grey area, different brand

The microinverter stage needs a UL 1741 listing to be taken seriously by any utility reviewing an interconnection request, and that's the first thing to check on the spec sheet or box before buying — our [UL 1741 vs IEEE 1547](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) explainer covers what that listing does and doesn't certify. Even with a listed inverter, plugging a Solarbank 2 into a wall outlet to feed your home's wiring runs into the same ambiguity we cover in our [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) guide: the code was written around hard-wired interconnections, and inspectors and utilities haven't settled on one consistent way to treat small plug-in battery-inverter combos. Some utilities are happy to treat a Solarbank 2 as behind-the-meter battery charging with no formal application needed; others want the same interconnection paperwork as a full rooftop system regardless of size. Check our [utility interconnection](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) guide and call your provider directly — don't assume either way based on what a seller's listing claims.

## Net metering vs net billing changes the value proposition

If your utility still runs traditional net metering, the Solarbank 2's battery buffering matters less, because exported surplus is credited at close to retail value anyway. If you're on a net-billing structure — California's NEM 3.0 being the best-known example — stored self-consumption is worth considerably more than anything you export, which is exactly the gap the Solarbank 2's battery is designed to close. Our [net metering vs net billing](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) comparison and [California guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-california) go into the state-specific math in more detail.

## How it stacks up against PowerStream

Functionally, the Solarbank 2 and EcoFlow's PowerStream solve the same problem in similar ways: both pair a small solar input with a battery and a grid-tied microinverter output, and both are aimed at self-consumption rather than full-house offset. The practical differences come down to ecosystem and stacking — Solarbank 2 is designed to expand with additional battery modules more cheaply than adding a second full EcoFlow power station, while PowerStream leans on EcoFlow's existing Delta-series batteries if you already own one. Neither is a substitute for a properly sized, interconnected rooftop system, and the same [is plug-in solar worth it](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-worth-it-us) logic applies to both: they're strongest as a supplement to an existing battery-backup setup or a way to trim a specific daytime load, not as a primary power source.

## Who it's a good fit for

The Solarbank 2 makes the most sense for homeowners or renters who want solar-charged battery backup without a hard-wired install, and who are comfortable navigating their utility's stance on plug-in interconnection themselves. If you're in a state with favorable net billing, restricted by an [HOA](/us/blog/hoa-rules-plug-in-solar-us), or just want to offset a specific circuit like a home office or fridge, it's a reasonable option — just go in with realistic expectations about scale, and confirm your utility's position before you buy rather than after.

### Does the Solarbank 2 need a licensed electrician to install?

The panel and battery setup is designed to be DIY-friendly and connects via a standard outlet, but if your utility requires formal interconnection paperwork, you may need an electrician to sign off even if they don't physically touch the wiring.

### Can I run a Solarbank 2 entirely off-grid?

Yes — many owners use it purely as a portable, solar-charged battery without ever connecting the AC output to their home wiring, which sidesteps the interconnection question entirely but also means you lose the automatic grid-tied self-consumption feature.

### Will this replace my electric bill?

No. At roughly 800W of inverter output and a few kilowatt-hours of storage, it offsets a modest daytime load — it isn't sized to replace a full rooftop solar and battery system.
