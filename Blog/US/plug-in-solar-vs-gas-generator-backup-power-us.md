When the power goes out, most American homeowners reach for the same answer their parents did: a gas generator parked by the garage. But a growing number of buyers are asking whether a plug-in solar kit paired with a battery can do some of that job instead — quieter, cheaper to run, and without a trip to the gas station mid-storm. The honest answer is that they solve different problems, and understanding which one fits your situation matters more than picking a "winner."

## What each one actually does

A gas or propane generator is built for one thing: producing a lot of power on demand, for as long as you can keep feeding it fuel. A whole-home standby unit can carry an entire house — HVAC, well pump, refrigerator, everything — for days, provided fuel supply holds up. That's a fundamentally different capability from a [plug-in solar](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-us-complete-guide) kit, which under [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) and [UL 1741](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) rules is a small-scale, grid-interactive or battery-backed system typically sized at 400–800W — enough for a refrigerator, routers, phone charging, and small loads, not a whole house.

Where a solar-plus-battery setup earns its keep is in the gap generators are worst at: short, frequent outages and continuous low-level backup that doesn't need anyone to go buy gas or run an engine in the driveway.

## Cost over time

Generators are cheap to buy and expensive to run. A portable gas unit costs $10–20 in fuel for roughly 8–10 hours of moderate use, and that adds up fast across a multi-day outage — plus oil changes, spark plugs, and the fact that stored gasoline degrades after a few months unless treated. A [plug-in solar kit](/us/blog/best-plug-in-solar-kits-us) with a battery has a higher upfront cost (often $600–1,500 depending on size) but effectively zero marginal fuel cost once installed, and the [federal tax credit](/us/blog/federal-tax-credit-plug-in-solar) can offset part of that initial outlay for qualifying systems.

For readers in [hurricane-prone states](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-hurricane-resilience-us) who might face several outages a year, the solar option's running-cost advantage compounds quickly. For someone who loses power once every few years, a generator's lower upfront cost and higher output per dollar of equipment may still make more sense.

## Noise and neighbors

This is where solar wins outright. A gas generator running for hours during a [winter storm outage](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-winter-storm-power-outage-prep-us) is loud enough to draw complaints in dense neighborhoods and most [HOA communities](/us/blog/hoa-rules-plug-in-solar-us) restrict where and when they can run. A plug-in solar and battery setup makes no noise at all, which also matters for apartment dwellers and anyone in a [manufactured or mobile home community](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-manufactured-mobile-homes-us) with tight setback rules.

## Reliability and weather

Generators don't care if it's cloudy — they'll run as long as fuel lasts, which is a real advantage during multi-day outages with poor weather. Solar output drops sharply on overcast days and is zero at night unless you've got battery reserve built up beforehand, so a solar-only backup plan is vulnerable to exactly the kind of extended, storm-driven outage it's often bought for. The practical fix most buyers land on is a hybrid approach: solar for routine, everyday resilience (keeping a fridge and router alive through brief outages) and a smaller backup generator or larger battery reserved for genuinely extended events.

## Maintenance

Generators need regular exercise cycles, fuel stabilizer, oil changes, and carburetor cleaning if left unused too long — a surprising number fail to start when actually needed because of poor maintenance. Plug-in solar systems are close to maintenance-free: occasional panel cleaning and checking connections, covered in our [RV and van life](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-for-rv-and-van-life) and general [troubleshooting](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-not-working-troubleshooting-us) guides, is about the extent of it.

## Which should you actually buy?

If your main concern is riding out brief, frequent outages quietly and cheaply, a plug-in solar and battery kit is the better fit, especially paired with [net metering or net billing](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) savings on sunny days when the grid is up. If you need to run a whole house, including HVAC and major appliances, through a multi-day outage regardless of weather, a generator — or a proper whole-home battery system installed by a licensed electrician — is still the more dependable choice. Plenty of households in storm-prone states end up with both.

### Can I run my refrigerator on plug-in solar during an outage?

Yes, provided you have battery storage paired with the panels — pure grid-tied plug-in solar without a battery stops producing usable backup power the moment the grid goes down, since most UL 1741-listed inverters are required to shut off during a grid outage for safety.

### Is a gas generator still worth buying if I already have solar?

For most households in storm-prone regions, yes — a small backup generator covers the gap during extended, cloudy-weather outages that battery reserve alone can't bridge, making the two systems complementary rather than competing.

### How long does a typical plug-in solar battery last during an outage?

It depends heavily on battery capacity and what you're running, but a typical 500–1000Wh battery paired with light loads (router, phone charging, small fridge) can last 8–24 hours without sun, and indefinitely longer if panels are still generating during the day.
