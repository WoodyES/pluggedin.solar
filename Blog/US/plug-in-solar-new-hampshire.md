New Hampshire combines two things that make small-scale solar unusually attractive: electricity rates well above the national average, and a net energy metering program that — unlike many neighboring states — still credits exported power at close to retail value. For a plug-in solar kit, that's a favorable combination even without full utility interconnection.

## Why New Hampshire's economics work in your favor

- **Retail rate:** New Hampshire residential electricity has consistently run **22–28 cents per kWh**, among the highest in New England and well above the roughly 17-cent national average.
- **No state sales tax:** Unlike most states, New Hampshire charges no sales tax on the kit itself, which shaves a meaningful amount off the upfront cost compared to buying the same hardware in Massachusetts or New York.
- **Net Energy Metering (NEM):** New Hampshire's investor-owned utilities (Eversource, Unitil, Liberty) operate under a Public Utilities Commission-regulated net metering structure that, for small systems, credits exports closer to retail rates than the net-billing wholesale credits now common in states like California. This matters less for an un-metered 800W plug-in kit that's mostly self-consumed, but it's a sign the regulatory environment favors small generators broadly.
- **Solar irradiance:** New Hampshire isn't the sunniest state, but seasonal variation is manageable — strong spring-through-fall output with a predictable winter dip, similar to [Vermont](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-vermont) and [Maine](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-maine).

## Where plug-in solar sits under NEC and state rules

Like the rest of the US, small plug-in solar kits (typically 200–800W with a UL 1741-listed microinverter) occupy a grey area under [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us), which governs interconnected power sources but was written with professionally installed, permitted PV systems in mind, not consumer plug-in kits.

New Hampshire doesn't have state-specific legislation explicitly authorizing or banning plug-in solar kits the way some states have begun drafting consumer-microgeneration rules. In practice:

- A kit using a **UL 1741-listed microinverter** plugged into a standard outlet is the de facto baseline for anyone buying one in the state.
- Formal **utility interconnection** paperwork is designed for larger, permitted systems — see our [utility interconnection guide](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) for what that process typically involves if you ever move beyond a small kit.
- Most New Hampshire homeowners running a single small panel-plus-microinverter kit do so without going through formal interconnection, similar to common practice described in our [state legality overview](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-in-my-state).

As always, we're not your electrician or your utility — check with Eversource, Unitil, or Liberty directly if you're unsure about your specific setup, and always use a **UL 1741**-listed inverter (never a non-listed import) to stay on the safer, more defensible side of the grey area. For the technical difference between listing standards, see [UL 1741 vs IEEE 1547](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us).

## Winter performance: the real New Hampshire variable

New Hampshire winters bring the two things that hurt solar output most — short days and snow cover. A few practical notes:

- Expect **output to drop by 60–75%** compared to summer peak between December and February.
- Snow-shedding matters more here than panel angle. A steeper mount angle (New Hampshire's latitude supports 45–55 degrees) helps snow slide off rather than accumulate.
- Many New Hampshire buyers treat their kit as a **seasonal supplement** — strong production April through October, modest contribution in winter — rather than expecting year-round flat output.

## HOA and rental considerations

New Hampshire has fewer HOA-governed developments than warmer-climate states, but where they exist, the same patchwork of rules applies as elsewhere in the country — see our [HOA rules guide](/us/blog/hoa-rules-plug-in-solar-us) for how to check your covenants before mounting anything visible from the street. Renters should also check lease terms before drilling into a deck rail or exterior wall; a free-standing ground-mount kit avoids most of that friction entirely.

## What to buy

For New Hampshire's climate and rate structure, prioritize:

- A **UL 1741-listed microinverter** (non-negotiable for safety and grey-area defensibility)
- A **steeper tilt mount** (45°+) to shed snow rather than let it sit
- A kit in the **400–800W range** — large enough to matter against 22–28¢/kWh rates, small enough to stay within typical plug-in kit norms

## New England in context

New Hampshire sits in good company regionally. [Vermont](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-vermont) and [Massachusetts](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-massachusetts) share similar winter output curves, though Massachusetts carries sales tax and a more actively regulated net metering cap that New Hampshire's smaller investor-owned utility footprint hasn't replicated in the same way. If you're comparing regional options before buying, our [full US guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-us-complete-guide) and [state-by-state legality overview](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-in-my-state) are good starting points, and our [best plug-in solar kits](/us/blog/best-plug-in-solar-kits-us) roundup covers current hardware options in detail.

### Does New Hampshire require a permit for a plug-in solar kit?

Small plug-in kits plugged into an existing outlet typically don't trigger the same permitting process as a full rooftop PV installation, which requires a building permit and formal utility interconnection. Formal permitting and interconnection apply to larger, hard-wired systems — check with your town's building department and your utility if you're unsure where your setup falls.

### Will a plug-in solar kit actually lower my Eversource bill?

Yes, proportionally. An 800W kit self-consuming through a sunny New Hampshire spring-to-fall stretch can offset a meaningful slice of daytime usage at 22–28¢/kWh rates — it won't zero out your bill, but the savings are more noticeable here than in lower-rate states.

### Does snow accumulation ruin plug-in solar panels?

No, but it does stop generation until it clears. A steeper mount angle and manually brushing off accumulated snow after storms will minimize dead days during New Hampshire winters.
