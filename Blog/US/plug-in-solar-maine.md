Maine isn't the state anyone pictures when they think about solar power — that's Arizona or California's job — but it has one factor working strongly in its favor: electricity is expensive. Combine that with a legal landscape that, like the rest of the country, treats small plug-in solar kits as a grey area under NEC Article 705 rather than something explicitly banned, and the calculation is more interesting than the sunshine-hours number alone suggests.

## Maine's electricity rates make the math work

Maine residential electricity rates have consistently sat among the highest in the continental US, often in the 24-30¢/kWh range depending on utility and time of year — well above the national average of roughly 16-17¢/kWh. Central Maine Power (CMP) and Versant Power are the two major utilities, and both have pushed through notable rate increases in recent years.

That matters more for plug-in solar than it does for a big rooftop system, because plug-in kits are small and generation-limited — every kWh you offset is worth more in a high-rate state than the same kWh would be worth somewhere cheap like Washington state. For the national framework on how this nets out, see our guide on [how much plug-in solar can save](/us/blog/how-much-can-plug-in-solar-save-us).

## Sun hours: lower than the Sun Belt, not negligible

Maine gets roughly 2,400-2,600 hours of sunshine annually depending on the region (coastal areas get more fog, interior areas get more clear days), putting it below Southern states but not dramatically below much of the rest of New England and the Midwest. Winters are short on daylight and often snowy, but spring through fall — particularly the long, clear days of a Maine summer — produce solid output from a well-sited 400-800W kit.

Snow load is the main seasonal consideration worth planning for: angled rigid panels generally shed snow reasonably well once they warm slightly in daylight, but a flat-mounted panel buried under snow for days generates nothing until it's cleared.

## NEC 705 and utility interconnection in Maine

Plug-in solar kits sit in the same legal grey zone in Maine as in every other US state — the National Electrical Code's Article 705 covers interconnected power production sources, and most small UL 1741-listed plug-in kits are designed to minimize this exposure, but formal utility interconnection agreements are built around larger, permitted systems, not 400-800W plug-in units. Our [NEC Article 705 explainer](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) covers what this grey area actually means in practice, and our [utility interconnection guide](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) covers how CMP and Versant generally handle small-scale generation inquiries if you want to be fully above-board.

## Net metering is limited compared to some states

Maine has net energy billing provisions, but unlike aggressive net-metering states, credit structures and program caps have shifted over time, and small plug-in kits in particular don't typically go through formal net metering applications at all — they're sized to offset your own consumption directly rather than export meaningfully to the grid. Our explainer on [net metering vs net billing](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) covers the national distinction if you're unclear on how this affects a small system.

## Dealing with Maine winters

A few practical notes specific to Maine's climate:

- **Mount panels at a steeper angle than you would in the South** — a steeper tilt (closer to 45-50°) helps shed snow faster and better matches Maine's lower winter sun angle.
- **Keep a snow brush or soft broom handy** specifically for clearing panels after storms — never use anything abrasive or metal that could scratch the glass.
- **Expect a real seasonal swing.** December/January generation can be a fraction of June/July output; budget your savings expectations around the annual average, not the summer peak.
- **Coastal fog is a factor** in towns like Portland, Bar Harbor, and along Route 1 — interior and western Maine locations often see more clear-sky days.

For general storm-season durability, our [storm-proofing guide](/us/blog/storm-proofing-plug-in-solar-us) covers wind and ice considerations relevant to Maine's nor'easters, which can bring damaging gusts well outside hurricane season.

## How Maine compares to the rest of New England

Maine's rate structure and climate put it closer to [Massachusetts](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-massachusetts) than to colder but cheaper-electricity states further inland. If you're deciding between a vacation property in Maine and one further south, or just curious how the economics shift, our [New York guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-new-york) is a useful comparison point for a northern state with a different rate and policy mix.

### Is plug-in solar worth it in Maine despite the lower sun hours?

Often yes, because Maine's electricity rates are among the highest in the continental US. Every kWh a small plug-in kit offsets is worth more there than in a cheaper-electricity, sunnier state, which partly compensates for fewer sun hours overall.

### Do I need a permit for plug-in solar in Maine?

Small plug-in kits generally don't go through the same permitting process as full rooftop systems, but rules sit in a legal grey area under NEC Article 705 nationwide. Check with CMP or Versant directly if you want written confirmation before installing, and never attempt a hardwired or whole-home setup without a licensed electrician and proper permits.

### How much snow affects plug-in solar output in Maine?

A panel buried under snow generates essentially nothing until cleared. Steeper mounting angles (45-50°) help shed snow faster than a shallow tilt, and a quick brush-off after storms restores generation immediately once the sun is out.
