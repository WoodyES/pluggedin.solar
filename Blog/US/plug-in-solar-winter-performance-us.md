Every plug-in solar owner hits the same moment in late autumn: the app that showed 1.8 kWh a day in August is suddenly showing 0.6 kWh, and the obvious question is whether something's broken. Usually nothing is. Winter output drop is the single most predictable thing about a small grid-tied solar setup, and understanding the shape of that drop — how much, why, and what (if anything) to do about it — saves a lot of unnecessary troubleshooting between November and February. If you're still new to how these systems work day to day, our [complete US guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-us-complete-guide) is a good starting point.

## How much output actually drops

For most of the continental US, a 400-800W plug-in kit will produce somewhere between 35% and 55% of its summer daily average once you're into December and January. The exact number depends heavily on latitude and local cloud cover more than temperature. A kit in Georgia or Arizona will hold up noticeably better through winter than the same kit in Michigan, Ohio, or the Pacific Northwest, where short days stack on top of persistent winter overcast.

Three things combine to cause the drop, and it helps to separate them because only one of them is a problem you can fix:

**Day length.** This is the biggest factor and the one you can do nothing about. Peak sun hours in December can be 40-50% shorter than in June, even under clear skies.

**Sun angle.** The sun sits much lower in the sky in winter, which matters a lot for panels mounted flat or at a shallow angle — a lot of the light hits the panel surface at a poor angle and is partially reflected rather than absorbed. This is the one factor you can meaningfully improve.

**Weather.** Winter storm systems bring more consecutive overcast days in most of the country than summer does, and a solar panel under heavy cloud can be producing 10-25% of its clear-sky output.

## The one thing worth doing: adjust the tilt angle

If your panel or mount allows angle adjustment — and most of the better 2-panel kits do — steepening the tilt for winter makes a real difference. The rule of thumb solar installers use is to set tilt roughly equal to your latitude plus 15 degrees for winter optimization, versus latitude minus 15 for summer. For someone in Denver (latitude ~39.7°), that's roughly a 55° tilt in winter versus a 25° tilt in summer — a genuinely different angle, not a minor tweak.

Not every plug-in kit supports this kind of adjustment out of the box. If you're shopping for a kit with winter performance in mind, check the mounting hardware before you buy — our [best mounting hardware guide](/us/blog/best-solar-panel-mounting-hardware-us) covers which brackets allow meaningful seasonal angle changes versus which ones are fixed.

## Should you take panels down for winter?

For most grid-tied setups under [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us), no — leaving them up and producing a reduced amount is almost always better than removing them and producing nothing, as long as the mounting is rated for your local wind and snow load. The exception is portable kits used seasonally (camping, RV, a kit that lives on a balcony rail only part of the year) — those genuinely can be stored to avoid weather exposure when you're not using them. If snow accumulation is a real factor where you live, a lightly tilted or vertical mount will shed snow far faster than one mounted nearly flat, which is another argument for steeper winter angles beyond the output benefit.

## Does cold weather itself hurt or help output?

This surprises people: cold weather actually helps panel efficiency, within reason. Solar panels, like most semiconductors, lose efficiency as they heat up — a panel at 95°F performs measurably worse per watt of sunlight than the same panel at 40°F. That's why you sometimes see a clear, cold, sunny December day outproduce, watt-for-watt, a hazy August afternoon. It's a secondary effect compared to day length and sun angle, but it's real, and it's part of why the output drop isn't quite as severe as day-length alone would suggest.

## Battery considerations if you're running one alongside

If your setup includes a battery — whether that's a connected power station or a standalone unit charged from the panel — winter is worth planning around in two ways. First, charge rate from the panel will be slower given reduced solar input, so don't expect the battery to top up at summer speed. Second, lithium batteries lose usable capacity in cold temperatures, so a unit stored outdoors or in an unheated garage may show less runway on paper than it did in summer even at the same state of charge. ## What this means for your expectations through February

If you're three months into ownership and watching the numbers fall, that's the system working as expected, not failing. The honest framing: winter is the season where plug-in solar contributes the least and matters the most for exactly that reason — a system that's already paid off most of its cost through summer and fall generation is now quietly offsetting whatever it can during the months electricity use (and sometimes rates) peak. If you want a sense of what your specific setup should be producing month to month, our [plug-in solar savings guide](/us/blog/how-much-can-plug-in-solar-save-us) breaks down realistic annual curves by region.

If output drops to near zero on a clear day, that's not winter — that's a fault. Check our [troubleshooting guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-not-working-troubleshooting-us) before assuming it's seasonal. And if you haven't yet looked into the [federal tax credit](/us/blog/federal-tax-credit-plug-in-solar) for your setup, winter — when you're less focused on day-to-day generation — is a reasonable time to get the paperwork in order before tax season.

### Why did my solar output drop by more than half going into winter?

Shorter days and a lower sun angle are the two biggest factors, and they combine — on a December day with 9 hours of daylight versus a June day with 15, and with the sun's energy striking a flat panel at a worse angle throughout, a 40-55% drop in a flat or low-tilt panel's output is completely normal, not a malfunction.

### Should I increase the tilt angle on my panels for winter?

Yes, if your mount allows it. Steepening the angle to roughly your latitude plus 15 degrees captures noticeably more of the low winter sun than leaving panels at a summer-optimized angle. It's the single highest-value seasonal adjustment you can make.

### Does snow or frost on the panel ruin generation for the day?

It can — a fully snow-covered panel produces essentially nothing until it clears, either by melting, sliding off a steep enough mount, or being manually brushed off (carefully, to avoid scratching the glass). A steeper winter tilt helps snow shed on its own rather than sitting and accumulating.
