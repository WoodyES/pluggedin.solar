Missouri doesn't come up often in solar conversations, but it's a reasonable candidate for a small plug-in kit — moderate sun, a genuinely deregulated patchwork of utility territories, and a climate where severe weather makes backup power generation genuinely useful rather than a nice-to-have. Here's what a Missouri household actually needs to know before buying one.

## Where plug-in solar sits legally in Missouri

Like most of the country, small grid-tied solar kits in the 200–800W range occupy a legal grey area in Missouri. There's no statewide law explicitly permitting or banning a plug-in microgenerator the way some proposals elsewhere describe, so the practical framework is the same one that applies nationally: [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) governs how any interconnected power source — including a small inverter plugged into a household outlet — interacts with the rest of a building's wiring, and a [UL 1741-listed inverter](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) is the baseline safety bar for anything you plug in. Our [state-by-state legality guide](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-in-my-state) covers how Missouri compares with its neighbors, and the short version is: nothing stops you from buying and using a compliant kit, but formal utility interconnection paperwork for a device this small is rarely pursued by homeowners or required by the utilities themselves.

## Ameren Missouri and Evergy: what their rules actually say

Most of the state is served by one of two major utilities — Ameren Missouri across the eastern half including St. Louis, and Evergy in the Kansas City metro area — plus a number of rural electric cooperatives. Both investor-owned utilities have interconnection processes designed around rooftop systems of several kilowatts, not 400–800W plug-in kits, and neither explicitly addresses portable, non-permanently-wired solar devices in their tariffs. In practice, a plug-in kit's export is small enough that it functions more like self-consumption than a grid-export arrangement — our [utility interconnection guide](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) walks through how this plays out generally, and the same logic applies whether you're an Ameren or Evergy customer. If you're on a rural cooperative, check their specific net metering tariff, since co-ops vary more than the big investor-owned utilities.

## Net metering vs. net billing in Missouri

Missouri still operates under a traditional net metering framework rather than the net-billing structures some states (notably California) have moved to, which is actually a point in favor of solar economics here, even at plug-in scale. Our [net metering vs. net billing comparison](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) explains the difference in plain terms. For a plug-in kit generating a few hundred kWh a year, the practical effect of net metering versus net billing is modest, but it's one less thing working against you compared to households in net-billing states.

## Storm resilience: the real reason to consider one here

Missouri sits at the edge of Tornado Alley and sees some of the most severe spring and summer storm activity in the country, along with ice storms that can knock out power for days in winter. A small plug-in solar kit paired with a portable power station won't run your whole house through an extended outage, but it can keep a fridge, a few lights, and phones charged during the kind of multi-day outage that follows a major ice storm or derecho — exactly the scenario our [storm-proofing guide](/us/blog/storm-proofing-plug-in-solar-us) and [hurricane and storm resilience guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-hurricane-resilience-us) cover in more detail, even though the latter is written with Gulf Coast storms in mind — the underlying setup is the same.

## Sizing and installing a kit in Missouri

Missouri's climate is humid continental, with hot, sunny summers that actually produce strong solar output despite the state's reputation for grey winters. A 400–800W kit mounted on a south-facing deck, garage roof, or ground frame will see its best production from April through September. Our [installation guide](/us/blog/how-to-install-plug-in-solar-us) covers the practical steps, and the [buying checklist](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-buying-checklist-us) is worth running through before you order, particularly around confirming your outlet circuit can safely handle the inverter's rated output.

## What it costs and the federal tax credit

Pricing for a plug-in kit runs from a couple hundred dollars for a basic 200W setup to $800–1,200 for a full 800W system with battery backup — see our [payback period guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-payback-period-us) for the numbers at Missouri's retail electricity rates, which sit close to the national average. Missouri residents may also be able to claim a portion of the [federal solar tax credit](/us/blog/federal-tax-credit-plug-in-solar) depending on how the system is purchased and installed; the guide covers which configurations typically qualify.

### Do I need Ameren or Evergy's permission to use a plug-in solar kit?

Neither utility has a formal process for a device this small, and most homeowners don't pursue formal interconnection paperwork for a plug-in kit. It's still worth a quick call to your utility if you want written confirmation, but most Missouri households simply use a compliant, UL 1741-listed kit without issue.

### Is Missouri a good state for plug-in solar given the storms?

Yes, for two different reasons — decent summer sun for generation, and genuine value as a storm-resilience tool given Missouri's exposure to tornadoes, derechos, and winter ice storms that can cause multi-day outages.

### How much can a plug-in solar kit realistically save in Missouri?

A well-sited 800W kit can offset a modest but real portion of a Missouri household's electricity use, typically saving somewhere in the range of $60–120 a year at current retail rates, with the added non-financial benefit of backup capability during storm outages.
