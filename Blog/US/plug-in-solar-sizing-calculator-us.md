The most common mistake US buyers make with plug-in solar isn't picking the wrong brand — it's picking the wrong size. Buy too small and you barely notice the savings; buy too large and you're paying for capacity your daytime usage can't actually absorb. Here's a straightforward way to work out the right wattage for your situation without guesswork.

## Step 1: Know your hard ceiling

Before anything else, understand the electrical limit you're working within. Under the [NEC Article 705 framework](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) that governs interconnected equipment, a standard 120V, 15A household circuit is limited to 80% continuous load, which works out to:

**15A × 120V × 80% = 1,440W continuous**

That's your absolute ceiling for a single standard outlet. In practice, almost every plug-in kit sold in the US tops out well under that, in the 400W-1,200W range, specifically to stay inside this limit with margin to spare. If a listing advertises something larger plugging into a normal wall outlet, treat that as a red flag rather than a selling point.

## Step 2: Estimate your daytime usage

Plug-in solar only offsets electricity you use *while the sun is generating* — typically 9am to 4pm depending on season and latitude. Battery-less systems don't store anything for evening use, so the calculation that matters isn't "how much electricity do I use per day," it's "how much do I use during daylight hours."

Walk through what's actually running in your home between roughly 9am and 4pm:

- Refrigerator (always running): ~0.3-0.5 kWh across that window
- Any home office equipment, laptops, monitors: ~0.3-0.6 kWh if someone works from home
- Fans or partial AC load: highly variable, 0.5-3+ kWh in hot climates
- Pool pump, if you have one: 1-3 kWh depending on run time — see our [pool pump guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-for-pool-pump-us) for sizing that specifically
- EV trickle charging: 1.5-2 kWh if charging during the day

Add these up for a rough daytime kWh target. Most households land somewhere between 1.5 and 5 kWh of genuinely offsettable daytime usage, depending on whether anyone's home and what's running.

## Step 3: Match wattage to that target

Here's roughly what each common system size generates on an average sunny day across most of the continental US (actual output varies by region and season — see our [400W vs 800W vs 1,200W comparison](/us/blog/400w-vs-800w-vs-1200w-plug-in-solar) for a fuller breakdown):

| System size | Good-sun daily output | Annual output (avg US sun) | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 400W | 1.5-2 kWh | ~480 kWh | Apartments, renters testing the concept, low daytime usage |
| 800W | 3-4 kWh | ~960 kWh | Most single-family homes with moderate daytime load |
| 1,200W | 4.5-6 kWh | ~1,440 kWh | Homes with AC, pool pumps, or EV daytime charging |

If your Step 2 estimate came out around 2 kWh, an 800W kit is probably overkill — a 400W setup will cover most of it at a lower upfront cost. If you landed closer to 5 kWh because of a pool pump or daytime AC use, 1,200W starts making more financial sense, assuming your outlet and circuit can handle it safely.

## Step 4: Account for your state's electricity rate

Sizing isn't just about watts — it's about whether the payback math works at your local rate. [Net metering vs net billing rules](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) and retail rates swing from roughly 14c/kWh in some states to over 39c/kWh in others, which changes the payback period for the same system dramatically. A 400W kit might pay for itself in 3 years in a high-rate state and take 8+ in a low-rate one. Check our [payback period guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-payback-period-us) to run the math for your specific rate.

## Step 5: Don't forget HOA and space constraints

Sizing on paper is only half the equation. If you're in a community with [HOA rules](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-and-your-hoa), some restrict panel placement or visible mounting hardware regardless of wattage, which can force you toward a smaller, more discreetly mounted kit even if the math says you'd benefit from more. Check those restrictions before buying rather than after, since returns on installed solar equipment are often limited.

## Putting it together

A reasonable rule of thumb: start with a 400W kit if you're renting, testing the concept, or have genuinely low daytime usage; go with 800W as the sensible default for most homeowners; and only step up to 1,200W if you've specifically identified a daytime load — AC, pool pump, EV charging — that justifies it. Our [complete US buying guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-us-complete-guide) covers the rest of the purchase decision once you've settled on a size.

### Can I just buy the biggest kit to be safe?

Not quite — beyond the NEC 80% continuous-load ceiling on a standard circuit, oversizing wastes money on capacity your daytime usage can't absorb without a battery, since excess generation beyond what you use simply isn't captured.

### Does the federal tax credit change the sizing math?

It can improve the payback period at any size, but it doesn't change how much you should buy — size for your actual daytime usage first, then check whether the [federal tax credit](/us/blog/federal-tax-credit-plug-in-solar) applies to your specific equipment and situation.

### What if my daytime usage estimate is wrong?

Start smaller rather than larger. A 400W kit is cheaper to test the concept with, and most retailers sell expansion panels so you can scale up to 800W later once you've seen real generation data against your actual usage.
