## Arkansas Gets More Sun Than Most People Assume

Arkansas isn't usually mentioned alongside the Sun Belt states, but its solar resource is genuinely solid — most of the state averages around 4.6 to 4.9 peak sun hours per day, with the southern and western counties edging slightly higher than the Ozark region in the north. A 600–800 watt plug-in kit mounted on a south-facing deck, patio, or ground frame in Little Rock, Fayetteville, or Fort Smith will typically generate somewhere in the 900–1,100 kWh per year range. That's comparable to Alabama or Georgia, and well ahead of most of the Midwest and Northeast.

Humidity and summer haze take a modest bite out of peak-season output, similar to the rest of the mid-South, but mild winters keep the generation curve flatter year-round than it would be further north.

## Entergy Arkansas, Rates, and Net Metering

Most of the state's population is served by Entergy Arkansas, with Arkansas Electric Cooperatives and a handful of municipal utilities covering the rest. Entergy's residential rates run around $0.11–0.13 per kWh — some of the lowest in the country — which means the per-kWh savings from any solar setup are smaller here than in higher-rate states like California or Massachusetts, even though the equipment itself is just as cheap to buy and run.

Arkansas does have a statewide net-metering framework, but it's aimed at interconnected, exporting systems — think full rooftop arrays going through a formal interconnection application. A compact plug-in kit generating 600–800W behind the meter, consumed instantly inside the house rather than exported to the grid, generally sits outside that framework entirely. See our [utility interconnection guide](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) and [net metering vs. net billing guide](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) for how that distinction plays out in practice — and confirm directly with Entergy or your local cooperative before assuming any setup is automatically exempt.

## NEC 705 and UL 1741 Still Apply

Like everywhere else in the US, plug-in solar in Arkansas sits in a grey area under [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us), which was written for interconnected rooftop systems and backup generators rather than small UL 1741-listed microinverter kits plugged into an ordinary outlet. Arkansas has adopted recent NEC editions with only minor state-level amendments, so the general guidance in our [UL 1741 vs. IEEE 1547 guide](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) applies here without much local variation. Staying with a UL 1741-listed microinverter, keeping total output under 800W, and making no permanent changes to your home's wiring are the three things that matter most for a defensible setup.

## HOA Rules in Arkansas's Growing Suburbs

Arkansas doesn't have a statewide solar-access law that overrides HOA covenants the way Texas or Florida do. That gives homeowners associations in newer subdivisions around Northwest Arkansas (Bentonville, Rogers, Springdale) and the Little Rock suburbs real latitude to restrict visible solar equipment, including small plug-in panels on patios, fences, and balconies. Our [HOA rules guide](/us/blog/hoa-rules-plug-in-solar-us) and [HOA deep dive](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-and-your-hoa) cover tactics that tend to work even without statewide protection — positioning panels away from street-facing elevations, choosing low-profile ground or rail mounts, and framing the request as a portable appliance rather than a fixed solar installation.

## Tornado Season and Severe Weather Resilience

Arkansas sits firmly inside the southern edge of Tornado Alley, with peak severe-weather season running March through May and a secondary risk window in the fall. Multi-day power outages after a major tornado outbreak or ice storm are a real possibility, and a plug-in panel paired with a portable power station gives households a practical way to keep essentials running — a refrigerator, phone and laptop charging, fans, and medical equipment — through an extended outage. Our [storm-proofing guide](/us/blog/storm-proofing-plug-in-solar-us) covers securing or bringing in portable panels ahead of a severe weather warning; during an active tornado watch, the only sensible move is to disconnect and store the panel indoors rather than trust any mounting hardware to survive straight-line winds or a direct hit.

## Federal Incentives Still Apply

Arkansas has no state-level solar tax credit, but the federal 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit applies regardless of state. Our [federal tax credit guide](/us/blog/federal-tax-credit-plug-in-solar) walks through what qualifies — for a plug-in kit costing $600–$800, that's $180–$240 back at tax time, which shortens an already short payback period even at Arkansas's low electricity rates. For a side-by-side look at whether a small kit or a full rooftop system makes more sense for your situation, our [plug-in vs. rooftop solar guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-vs-rooftop-solar-us) is worth reading before you commit either way.

### Does Entergy Arkansas charge extra for small plug-in solar kits?

Entergy's net-metering rules target interconnected, exporting rooftop systems rather than small behind-the-meter plug-in kits that don't export power. A non-exporting 600–800W kit generally falls outside that framework, but it's worth confirming directly with Entergy or your local cooperative before installing, since rules can vary by provider.

### Can my Arkansas HOA stop me from installing a plug-in solar panel?

Possibly — Arkansas lacks a statewide solar-access law protecting homeowners from HOA restrictions. Many HOAs will still approve a low-profile, non-street-facing setup when it's presented as a portable appliance rather than a fixed installation; see our HOA guide for the approach that tends to succeed.

### Is plug-in solar worth it in Arkansas given the state's low electricity rates?

Yes, generally. Arkansas's sub-$0.13/kWh rates mean smaller per-kWh savings than higher-cost states, but good sun hours and the federal tax credit still bring payback on a typical kit into the 5–7 year range, with the equipment continuing to produce well beyond that.
