South Carolina is an easy state to underestimate for solar. It doesn't get the national attention that California or Hawaii get, but it has strong year-round sun, a coastline that runs from Myrtle Beach down through Charleston to Hilton Head, and one of the most generous state-level solar tax credits in the country for anyone who eventually moves up to a full rooftop system. For a small plug-in kit specifically, none of the state incentives apply directly — but the utility landscape, hurricane exposure, and heavy HOA presence along the coast all matter.

## A state split between two utilities

Most of the Midlands and Lowcountry — including Columbia and Charleston — is served by Dominion Energy South Carolina (the former SCE&G), while Duke Energy Carolinas and Duke Energy Progress cover much of the Upstate and Pee Dee regions, with Santee Cooper and several electric cooperatives filling in the rest, particularly in rural counties. As in most states, a small plug-in kit running under roughly 800W on a [UL 1741-listed inverter](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) sits in the same regulatory gray area it occupies nationally under [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) — not explicitly legislated, but widely sold and used, and distinct from the formal [utility interconnection](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) process built for grid-exporting rooftop systems.

South Carolina's net energy metering rules (under state legislation dating back to Act 236, later revised) apply to systems that go through formal interconnection review with capacity caps by utility. A plug-in kit that simply offsets your own daytime load and doesn't meaningfully export typically doesn't trigger that process at all — see our [net metering vs. net billing guide](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) if you're weighing a plug-in kit against eventually adding a larger system that would.

## The rate and payback math

South Carolina's average residential electricity rate runs a little below the national average, generally in the **13-15 cents per kWh** range depending on utility territory, with Dominion's Lowcountry rates typically a shade higher than Duke's Upstate rates. That's a moderate picture for plug-in solar payback:

| System size | Annual generation (SC average sun) | Annual saving (~14c/kWh) | Payback ($700 kit) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 400W | 570-640 kWh | $80-$90 | 8-9 years |
| 800W | 1,100-1,250 kWh | $154-$175 | 4-4.5 years |

Coastal counties (Charleston, Beaufort, Horry) get slightly more consistent sun than the Upstate around Greenville and Spartanburg, though the gap is small enough that it shouldn't drive your buying decision on its own. Our [North Carolina guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-north-carolina) covers a similar rate and generation profile just across the state line if you're comparing the two.

## Hurricane season on the coast

South Carolina's coastline runs the same June-through-November Atlantic hurricane season as the rest of the Southeast, and Charleston, Myrtle Beach and the Hilton Head area have all taken direct or near-direct hits in recent years (Matthew in 2016, Irma and Dorian since). If you're anywhere near the coast, two things matter more here than in a landlocked state:

- **Bring panels in before a storm.** A lightweight plug-in panel becomes a hazard in high wind if it isn't weighted down or brought indoors. Our [hurricane resilience guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-hurricane-resilience-us) and [storm-proofing guide](/us/blog/storm-proofing-plug-in-solar-us) both cover the pre-storm checklist.
- **Plan for multi-day outages afterward.** Coastal SC counties routinely lose power for several days after a direct hit. A plug-in kit paired with a portable power station won't run central air, but it will keep phones, a fridge and medical equipment going through the first 48 hours, which is often what matters most.

## HOAs and vacation rentals along the coast

A large share of South Carolina's coastal growth — Myrtle Beach, Hilton Head, Kiawah, and much of the Charleston-area suburbs — sits inside HOA-governed communities, many with strict rules on anything visible from the street or a shared amenity area. This is especially relevant if you own a vacation rental property along the coast, where an HOA or rental management agreement may have its own restrictions beyond the standard covenant. Check our [HOA rules guide](/us/blog/hoa-rules-plug-in-solar-us) before buying — ground-mounted kits tucked behind a privacy fence or on a rear patio tend to draw far less scrutiny than anything mounted facing the street.

## What to buy

For most South Carolina households, a standard 800W plug-in kit with a genuinely UL 1741-listed microinverter is the sensible starting point — enough to meaningfully cut daytime load without tripping any formal interconnection threshold in most utility territories.

### Does South Carolina's state solar tax credit apply to a plug-in kit?

No — the state's 25% solar tax credit is built for permitted, professionally installed rooftop systems that go through formal interconnection, not small plug-in kits. It's worth knowing about if you later consider a full system, but it doesn't change the math on an 800W plug-in kit today.

### Which utility territory am I in — does it matter for a plug-in kit?

Dominion Energy South Carolina covers most of the Midlands and Lowcountry, while Duke Energy Carolinas and Duke Energy Progress cover much of the Upstate and Pee Dee. For a small kit that only offsets your own load rather than exporting to the grid, the practical difference between territories is minor — the rate difference matters more for payback than any interconnection rule.

### What should I do with a plug-in kit before a hurricane?

Bring portable panels indoors or into a garage as soon as a storm watch is issued for your area, and make sure any ground-mounted or patio hardware is properly secured. See our [hurricane resilience guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-hurricane-resilience-us) for a full pre-storm checklist.
