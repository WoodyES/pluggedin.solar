Anyone shopping for a US-market plug-in solar kit runs into two acronyms: **UL 1741** and **IEEE 1547**. Both govern how a grid-connected inverter must behave. Neither is optional. Getting them mixed up is a common source of confusion for first-time buyers.

## What UL 1741 covers

UL 1741 is the American safety standard for inverters, converters, controllers and interconnection system equipment for distributed energy resources. It's the certification you look for on a product's label — "UL 1741 Listed" means the inverter has been tested by an accredited lab against the standard's electrical safety, thermal, and grid-interaction requirements.

The two supplements that matter for modern plug-in kits:

- **UL 1741 SA** (Supplement A) — grid support functions like anti-islanding, voltage/frequency ride-through, and power quality. Required for any grid-tied installation post-2017 in most states.
- **UL 1741 SB** (Supplement B) — updated Cal Rule 21 / IEEE 1547-2018 compliance for California and increasingly other progressive states.

For the wider US regulatory picture see [NEC Article 705 explained](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us).

## What IEEE 1547 covers

IEEE 1547 is the technical **standard** that defines HOW the inverter should behave — voltage, frequency, ramp rates, anti-islanding timing, ride-through curves. UL 1741 SA/SB test **that** the inverter complies with IEEE 1547.

Think of it this way: IEEE 1547 is the recipe, UL 1741 is the taste test.

## Why the distinction matters when shopping

Some cheap imported inverters claim "IEEE 1547 compliant" without being UL 1741 listed. That means the manufacturer says they conform to the technical spec — but no accredited lab has verified it. Utility inspectors reject these routinely. **Only UL 1741 listing (with the SA or SB supplement) counts as certified for US utility interconnection.**

Hoymiles, Enphase, APsystems and EcoFlow's US microinverters all carry UL 1741 SA/SB listings. Verify on the actual product page before buying.
