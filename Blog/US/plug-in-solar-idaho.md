Idaho rarely comes up in national solar conversations, overshadowed by its Mountain West neighbors Utah and Colorado, but it has a genuinely interesting profile for plug-in solar: strong sun resource across the southern part of the state, among the lowest electricity rates in the country, and winters cold and snowy enough that seasonal planning matters more than it does for most buyers.

## Idaho's Solar Resource

Southern Idaho — Boise, the Treasure Valley, Twin Falls and the Snake River Plain — sees around 5.0-5.4 peak sun hours per day on average, helped by the region's high elevation and generally dry, clear climate outside of winter. Northern Idaho, around Coeur d'Alene and Sandpoint, runs noticeably lower due to more cloud cover and mountain shading.

A 600-800W plug-in kit in the Boise area typically generates **850-1,050 kWh per year**. At Idaho's average residential rate of roughly $0.11-0.12/kWh — among the lowest in the US — that works out to $95-125 in annual savings, giving a payback of around 6-7 years on a typical kit. That's longer than in high-rate states like California or Hawaii, but Idaho's low rates also mean a plug-in kit is cheap insurance against future price increases rather than a dramatic bill-cutter today.

## Where Plug-In Solar Fits Legally

Idaho has no dedicated statute specifically regulating "plug-in solar" as its own category, so small kits fall under the same general grey area seen across most of the US. [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) governs interconnected power production sources at the national code level, and UL 1741-listed microinverters are designed to operate safely within that framework. For the current state-by-state picture, see our [legal status guide](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-in-my-state).

## Utility Territory: Idaho Power, Avista, and Rocky Mountain Power

Most of southern and central Idaho, including Boise, is served by Idaho Power. Northern Idaho is largely Avista territory, and a portion of eastern Idaho falls under Rocky Mountain Power. All three have interconnection processes designed around larger rooftop systems, and none currently has a formally defined pathway for small plug-in kits — which is typical nationally rather than an Idaho-specific gap. Our general [utility interconnection guide](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) covers what to expect when you contact your provider.

## Net Metering: Idaho Power's Phased-Out Program

This is the detail that matters most for Idaho buyers. Idaho Power phased out traditional 1:1 net metering for new residential solar customers starting in 2024, moving new applicants to an "on-bill credit" structure that compensates exported power at a lower rate than the retail price. Existing net metering customers were grandfathered for a transition period. For a small plug-in kit running well under the thresholds that trigger formal interconnection review, this matters less in practice — self-consumption, not export, is where most of the savings come from on an 800W system. Our breakdown of [net metering vs net billing](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) explains why export compensation rates matter far less for small kits than for full rooftop arrays.

## Winter Is the Real Design Constraint

Idaho's winters are the factor that most distinguishes it from sunnier, milder states. Boise averages real snowfall most winters, and the mountainous parts of the state see far more. A few things to plan around:

- **Snow load on ground-mounted frames.** Choose a mount rated for at least light seasonal snow accumulation, and don't let panels sit buried for days — even a thin, even layer of snow blocks nearly all generation.
- **Steeper winter sun angle.** Idaho's latitude (roughly 42-49°N) means a noticeably lower winter sun path than Texas or Florida. A panel angled for summer performance will underperform badly from November through February unless you adjust the tilt — our [panel angle guide](/us/blog/where-to-put-plug-in-solar-panels-us) covers the general principles.
- **Freeze-thaw cycling on connectors.** Repeated freezing and thawing can work cable glands loose over a season; check connections each fall before the first hard freeze.

## HOA Rules in Idaho

Idaho doesn't have a strong statewide solar-access law overriding HOA covenants the way some states do, so community restrictions on visible panels — including balcony or yard-mounted plug-in kits — can be enforceable in many Idaho subdivisions, particularly newer developments around the Boise suburbs. Check your covenant before buying; our [HOA rules guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-and-your-hoa) covers what's typically negotiable even where an outright ban exists.

## The Federal Tax Credit Still Applies

Idaho has no state-level solar tax credit for small plug-in kits, but the federal 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit applies regardless of state, provided the equipment qualifies. On a $600 kit, that works out to $180 back when filing. See our [federal tax credit guide](/us/blog/federal-tax-credit-plug-in-solar) for current eligibility details.

## Buying and Installing

Run through our general [plug-in solar buying checklist](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-buying-checklist-us) before ordering — inverter certification, panel wattage sizing, and mount compatibility all matter, and the snow-load and tilt-angle considerations above are more important in Idaho than in most of the country.

## Bottom Line

Idaho's solar resource is solid, especially in the Boise area, but its low electricity rates mean the payback math is slower than in high-cost states — think years, not months, of extra patience compared to California. Where Idaho genuinely stands out is winter planning: get the tilt angle and snow-load mounting right, and a plug-in kit will perform reliably through a Mountain West winter.

### Is plug-in solar legal in Idaho?

There's no dedicated state law specifically addressing plug-in solar kits. Most small UL 1741-listed systems operate under the general NEC Article 705 framework used across most of the US. Check your utility's interconnection rules and any HOA covenant before installing.

### Does Idaho Power still offer net metering?

Idaho Power phased out traditional 1:1 net metering for new residential customers starting in 2024, replacing it with a lower on-bill export credit. This affects export compensation more than self-consumption savings, so it matters less for small plug-in kits than for full rooftop systems.

### How much does snow affect a plug-in solar kit in Idaho?

Significantly if left unmanaged — even a thin, even layer of snow can block nearly all generation. Use a mount rated for seasonal snow loads and plan to clear panels after snowfall during the winter months for consistent output.
