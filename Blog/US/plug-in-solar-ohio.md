Ohio doesn't get the sunshine headlines that California or Arizona do, but it has something arguably more useful for plug-in solar adoption: a genuine state net metering law already on the books, moderate electricity rates that keep rising, and large utility territories where a lot of homeowners are simply looking for a low-effort way to trim their bill.

## Sun hours and realistic output

Ohio averages around 4.0–4.3 peak sun hours per day statewide, with the northern Lake Erie counties (Cleveland, Toledo) running slightly lower due to lake-effect cloud cover in winter, and the southern counties (Cincinnati, Athens) a touch higher. That's noticeably less than Texas or California, but still enough to make a plug-in kit worthwhile.

A 600W plug-in system in Ohio will typically generate 750–900 kWh per year on an unshaded, south-facing balcony or fence mount. An 800W kit pushes that to roughly 950–1,150 kWh annually. Winters are genuinely weak — expect 20–40 kWh a month from November through February — but April through September more than makes up for it, often exceeding 130 kWh a month in peak summer.

## Electricity rates and the net metering picture

Ohio's average residential rate sits around $0.13–$0.15 per kWh as of 2026, which is close to the national average — not California-level savings, but enough for a plug-in kit to pay for itself in a reasonable window. At $0.14/kWh, an 800W system generating 1,000 kWh/year is worth about $140 annually, putting payback on a $1,000–$1,200 kit at roughly 7–9 years before factoring in the [federal tax credit](/us/blog/federal-tax-credit-plug-in-solar).

Ohio Revised Code already requires investor-owned utilities to offer net metering to customer-generators, crediting exported energy at the utility's standard retail rate for systems under the relevant capacity threshold. That's a genuinely favorable starting point compared to states still running net billing schemes — see our explainer on [net metering vs net billing](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) for why that distinction matters so much to the economics.

The catch is that plug-in solar's interconnection status is still being worked out utility-by-utility. AEP Ohio, Duke Energy Ohio, Dominion Energy Ohio, and Toledo Edison / Cleveland Illuminating (part of FirstEnergy) all have interconnection processes built around rooftop systems with a licensed installer — not a homeowner plugging a kit into a wall outlet. Small kits under the [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) threshold are being sold and used across Ohio today, but formal utility sign-off for a plug-in-specific fast-track process isn't yet standardized statewide.

## Utility territories in Ohio

Most Ohio homeowners fall under one of four investor-owned utilities:

- **AEP Ohio** — Columbus and central/eastern Ohio
- **Duke Energy Ohio** — Cincinnati and the southwest
- **Dominion Energy Ohio** — Cleveland-area gas, with electric split among others
- **FirstEnergy (Illuminating Company / Toledo Edison / Ohio Edison)** — Cleveland, Toledo, Akron, and the north/northeast

A handful of municipal utilities (Cleveland Public Power, Hamilton, Painesville) and rural electric cooperatives cover smaller pockets, and their interconnection rules can differ meaningfully from the investor-owned utilities above — always check your specific provider's small-generator policy before installing anything that exports to the grid.

## UL 1741 and inverter compliance

Whichever kit you buy, the inverter should carry [UL 1741 listing](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) — this is the baseline safety and grid-interaction certification that most Ohio utilities will expect to see referenced if you ever need to have a conversation with them about your setup. A kit using a UL 1741-listed micro-inverter (Hoymiles and Enphase-based products both commonly qualify) is a much easier conversation than an unlisted import.

## Weather: lake-effect snow, humidity, and storms

Northern Ohio's lake-effect snow belt (Cleveland east through Erie County) sees genuinely heavy winter cloud cover, which drags down winter output more than the sun-hour averages alone suggest — budget for a weaker December through February than a straight statewide average would imply. Panel snow load is rarely a structural issue for lightweight plug-in panels, but a snow-covered panel generates essentially nothing until cleared, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles are worth checking your mounting hardware against each spring.

Ohio's spring and summer bring genuine severe weather risk — tornadoes and derechos aren't rare in the Ohio Valley and central corridor. The portability of plug-in solar is a real advantage here: unlike fixed rooftop panels, you can bring a plug-in kit inside ahead of a severe thunderstorm warning, which is exactly the kind of resilience angle we cover in our [storm-proofing guide](/us/blog/storm-proofing-plug-in-solar-us).

## HOAs and renters

Ohio doesn't currently have a statewide HOA solar-access law as strong as some states', so HOA restrictions on visible balcony or yard equipment can still apply — check your covenants before mounting anything visible from the street. Renters should also confirm with a landlord before drilling into a balcony rail or fence; a clamp-mounted kit that requires no permanent fixing is the safer starting point, and our general [buying checklist](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-buying-checklist-us) covers what to look for either way.

## Is plug-in solar worth it in Ohio?

For the right household — south-facing exposure, no HOA restriction, and a willingness to wait out a 7–9 year payback — yes, particularly once the federal tax credit is factored in. It's not the fastest payback in the country, but Ohio's existing net metering law and large investor-owned utility footprint make it a more predictable environment than states still without any net metering framework at all. ### Does Ohio have net metering for plug-in solar?

Ohio's state net metering law applies to customer-generators broadly and predates plug-in solar specifically, so kits are covered in principle, but formal plug-in-specific interconnection fast-tracks vary by utility and aren't yet standardized statewide.

### How much does a plug-in solar kit save in Ohio?

At Ohio's average $0.13–$0.15/kWh rate, an 800W kit generating around 1,000 kWh/year is worth roughly $130–$150 annually, before any federal tax credit.

### Which Ohio utility is most plug-in solar friendly?

AEP Ohio and Duke Energy Ohio have the most established small-generator interconnection processes among the state's investor-owned utilities, though none currently offer a plug-in-specific fast track — check your utility's current small-generator policy directly before installing.
