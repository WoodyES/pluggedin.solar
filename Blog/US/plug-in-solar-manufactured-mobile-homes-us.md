Manufactured and mobile homes make up a bigger share of US housing than most people realize — roughly 1 in 15 occupied homes nationally, and a much higher share in states like Florida, Texas, South Carolina and Mississippi. Many sit on leased lots inside manufactured home communities rather than owned land, which changes almost everything about how a solar project works compared with a conventional single-family rooftop install. Plug-in solar, precisely because it doesn't require roof penetrations, a permanent electrical tie-in, or a long-term lease commitment, fits this housing type better than full rooftop solar does in most cases. Here's what actually matters.

## Why rooftop solar rarely makes sense on a manufactured home

Manufactured home roofs are typically lighter-gauge steel or low-slope construction that wasn't engineered to carry the weight and wind-load profile of a conventional rooftop solar array. Installers who will take on the job at all often charge a premium for specialized mounting hardware, and many simply decline the work. On top of that, if you're on a leased lot — the norm in most manufactured home communities — you don't own the land underneath the home, which makes a $15,000–20,000 rooftop investment a hard sell even before the structural questions come up.

A plug-in kit sidesteps both problems. A [400W or 800W panel](/us/blog/400w-vs-800w-vs-1200w-plug-in-solar) on a ground-mount frame or a lightweight rack attached to a skirting rail draws no load from the home's roof structure at all, and because it's portable, it goes with you if you relocate the home or move communities — which happens more often in this housing segment than in conventional single-family ownership. Our [complete guide to how plug-in solar works at 120V](/us/blog/how-plug-in-solar-works-us-120v) covers the basic electrical picture if you're new to the category.

## Park rules: the real gatekeeper

For most manufactured home residents, the practical obstacle isn't the utility or the NEC — it's the community's own rules. Manufactured home communities typically operate under a rental agreement or community rulebook similar to an HOA's CC&Rs, and many were written decades before plug-in solar existed, so they're often silent on it rather than explicitly banning it. That silence cuts both ways: it means you're less likely to run into an outright prohibition, but it also means you should ask park management directly rather than assume.

The approach is the same one we recommend for [HOA communities](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-and-your-hoa): put the request in writing, describe the system as non-permanent and fully removable, and note that it involves no roof penetration and no permanent wiring change. Residents in the 29+ states with solar access statutes have additional legal footing if a park operator pushes back, though manufactured home community agreements don't always fall squarely under the same statutory language as a typical HOA — when in doubt, a quick read of your specific lease or community rules beats assuming the law automatically applies.

## Wiring and the panel question

Most manufactured homes built since the 1990s are wired to a standard that comfortably handles a 120V plug-in solar kit through an ordinary dedicated outdoor or garage outlet, the same as any site-built home. The things worth checking before you buy: the age and condition of the home's electrical panel, since older units (especially pre-1976 homes, which predate the HUD manufactured housing code) sometimes have undersized or outdated panels that are worth having inspected regardless of solar; and whether the outlet you intend to use is on a circuit shared with anything sensitive to backfeed, which a licensed electrician can check in a few minutes. Our [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) guide covers the interconnection rules that apply regardless of home type, and [utility interconnection](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) covers what, if anything, you need to tell your power company.

## Storm and wind resilience matters more here

Manufactured homes are disproportionately concentrated in hurricane- and tornado-prone states, and a loose panel becomes a much more dangerous projectile around a lightweight home than around a heavier site-built one. If you're in Florida, the Gulf Coast, or anywhere in Tornado Alley, read the [storm-proofing guide](/us/blog/storm-proofing-plug-in-solar-us) before you mount anything, and plan on bringing panels inside or securing them firmly ahead of any named storm. The [Florida guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-florida) has state-specific detail if that's where you're located, including how manufactured home communities there typically handle solar requests.

## Is it worth it financially?

Manufactured home utility bills are often a larger share of household budget than for conventional homeowners, which makes even modest solar savings proportionally more meaningful. A single 800W kit running close to full output for a good chunk of the day can meaningfully offset a window AC unit or refrigerator load — see [how much plug-in solar can save](/us/blog/how-much-can-plug-in-solar-save-us) for realistic numbers across different system sizes and electricity rates. Because the equipment is portable and requires no structural work, the payback math is also less sensitive to how long you expect to stay in the home, which matters in a housing segment where relocation is more common than for conventional owners.

### Can I install plug-in solar if I rent my manufactured home lot?

Yes, in most cases. You own the equipment, not the land, so a portable plug-in kit doesn't require the kind of long-term capital commitment that rooftop solar would on leased land. Check your community's rules first, since lot-rental agreements can restrict exterior additions even when they don't mention solar specifically, and get anything unclear confirmed in writing with park management.

### Will plug-in solar void my manufactured home's warranty?

A correctly sized, non-penetrating ground-mount kit plugged into an existing outlet shouldn't affect a manufactured home's structural or electrical warranty, since nothing is altered on the home itself. If you're considering anything roof-mounted, check with the manufacturer first — most manufactured home roof warranties explicitly exclude added rooftop loads they didn't engineer for.

### What size plug-in solar kit is realistic for a manufactured home?

Most manufactured homes have enough outdoor space around the unit or within the lot for a single 400–800W ground-mount kit, which is also the range most compliant, widely available kits fall into. Larger multi-panel setups are possible on bigger lots but start to raise the same interconnection and park-approval questions as a conventional residential system, so bigger isn't always simpler here.
