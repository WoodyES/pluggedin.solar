Tiny homes sit in an odd gap in the US solar market. They're too small, too mobile, or too code-ambiguous for most rooftop installers to bother with, but they draw real, metered electricity like any other dwelling — often from a single 30A or 50A shore-power style connection. That combination makes small plug-in solar kits a surprisingly good match, provided you understand what a tiny home's electrical system can and can't absorb. Here's how it actually plays out.

## Why tiny homes are a different case from a regular house

A typical site-built or [manufactured mobile home](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-manufactured-mobile-homes-us) draws on a standard 100-200A household panel designed for a full suite of large appliances. A tiny home, by contrast, is frequently wired closer to how an RV is wired — a single shore power inlet feeding a much smaller subpanel, sized for a handful of lower-draw appliances rather than central air and an electric range. That's good news for plug-in solar: a 400-800W kit represents a meaningfully larger share of a tiny home's typical daytime draw than it would in a full-size house, so the percentage offset is often better even though the absolute kWh generated is identical. If you've read our [guide to how plug-in solar works at 120V](/us/blog/how-plug-in-solar-works-us-120v), the fundamentals here are the same — this is really about sizing, not a different technology.

## Roof space is usually the real constraint, not the electrics

Most tiny homes on wheels have a roof under 300 square feet, much of which is already claimed by vents, AC units, skylights, or simply isn't flat enough to mount a rigid panel safely. That's one reason tiny home owners increasingly lean on ground-mounted or A-frame kits set up beside the home rather than on top of it — the same approach covered in our [RV and van life guide](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-for-rv-and-van-life), which overlaps heavily with tiny home use even though the vehicles themselves are different. A freestanding kit also sidesteps roof penetration concerns entirely, which matters if the tiny home is financed, insured, or resold — modifying the roof envelope of a tiny home on a trailer chassis is a bigger structural question than it is on a conventional house.

## Where a tiny home sits on a foundation, different rules apply

Tiny homes permanently sited on a foundation and connected to utility service are treated by most jurisdictions like any other small dwelling, which means a grid-tied system technically falls under [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) and UL 1741 inverter listing requirements, same as a full-size house. In practice, the plug-in kits most tiny home owners actually buy are sized and used the same way RV kits are — feeding a limited circuit or a portable power station rather than tying into the home's main panel — which keeps them in the same informal grey-area territory we cover across the rest of this site. If your tiny home sits in a community with an HOA-style set of covenants, our [HOA rules guide](/us/blog/hoa-rules-plug-in-solar-us) and [plug-in solar and your HOA](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-and-your-hoa) piece are worth reading before you mount anything visible from the street.

## Sizing a kit to a tiny home's actual loads

The appliances that matter most in a tiny home are usually a compact fridge, LED lighting, a laptop or two, phone charging, and maybe a small space heater or window AC unit for a few hours a day. A single 400W panel paired with a modest power station comfortably covers the first group; stepping up to an 800W array starts to meaningfully offset the fridge and lighting load across most of the daylight hours, which is the same sizing logic in our [400W vs 800W vs 1200W comparison](/us/blog/400w-vs-800w-vs-1200w-plug-in-solar). Space heaters and AC units are the budget-breakers — both draw far more than a small kit can supply directly, so plan to run those from grid or propane and let solar handle everything else.

## Financing, insurance, and resale considerations

Tiny homes are frequently financed as RVs or personal property rather than real estate, which affects what insurers and lenders will sign off on for permanent modifications. A plug-in or freestanding kit that can be unplugged and taken with the home (or removed before a sale) tends to cause fewer headaches than a hard-mounted rooftop array wired into a fixed subpanel. If you're weighing the economics more broadly, our [plug-in solar vs rooftop solar](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-vs-rooftop-solar-us) comparison and [is plug-in solar worth it](/us/blog/is-plug-in-solar-worth-it-us) piece both apply directly to the smaller-footprint math a tiny home runs on.

### Can a plug-in solar kit fully power a tiny home off-grid?

For most tiny homes, no — not year-round. It can comfortably cover lighting, electronics, and a compact fridge on sunny days, but heating, cooling, and well pumps typically need a larger dedicated off-grid system or a grid/generator backup.

### Do tiny homes need a permit for a plug-in solar kit?

Usually not for a portable, freestanding kit that isn't wired into the home's fixed electrical system — but check your specific municipality or RV park rules, especially if the tiny home is on a permanent foundation.

### Is a plug-in kit better than a small rooftop system for a tiny home?

For most trailer-based tiny homes, yes — it avoids roof penetrations, structural load questions, and the resale complications of a hard-mounted array, at the cost of taking up some ground footprint beside the home.
