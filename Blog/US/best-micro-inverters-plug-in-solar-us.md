Almost every plug-in solar kit sold in the US is really two products bundled together: one or two solar panels, and a micro-inverter that converts their DC output into grid-matching AC before it reaches your outlet. The panels get most of the marketing attention, but the micro-inverter is the part doing the safety-critical work — and it's the part that determines whether a kit is even legal to plug in under [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us) in the first place. This guide compares the micro-inverters that actually show up inside US plug-in kits and what to check before buying.

## Why the micro-inverter matters more than the panel

A solar panel is a commodity part — two 400W monocrystalline panels from different brands perform almost identically. The micro-inverter is where compliance, safety, and real-world reliability live. It has to detect grid voltage and frequency, synchronize its output phase, and disconnect instantly if the grid goes down (anti-islanding protection) so it doesn't energize a de-energized line that a utility worker might be touching. That functionality is exactly what [UL 1741](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) testing certifies, and it's the single most important spec to verify before buying any kit, plug-in or otherwise.

## What to check before buying

**UL 1741 listing.** This should be stated explicitly in the product listing or spec sheet, not implied. If a seller can't point to a UL file number, treat that as a red flag — our guide on [what UL 1741 vs IEEE 1547 actually means](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) explains the difference between the two standards you'll see referenced.

**Output cap matching your plan.** Most residential-plug kits top out around 600-800W of inverter output, which keeps them within the range many utilities treat informally rather than requiring a full interconnection application. Going bigger isn't necessarily better — our [sizing calculator](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-sizing-calculator-us) helps match inverter capacity to your actual household draw rather than buying more than you'll use.

**App monitoring and per-panel data.** Micro-inverters report generation data individually rather than as one combined string total, which matters if you ever want to diagnose a shading issue on one panel versus a wiring fault. Look for a companion app with historical data export, not just a live dashboard — useful when you're trying to work out if a dip in output is weather, shading, or a fault.

**Weatherproofing rating.** An IP65 or higher rating is standard for anything mounted outdoors long-term. Check this separately from the panel's own waterproofing — the inverter housing is the more failure-prone component in wet climates.

**Cooling method.** Passive (fanless) convective cooling is the norm for small plug-in-scale inverters and tends to be more reliable long-term than anything with a fan, since there's no moving part to fail.

## Common micro-inverter brands in US plug-in kits

**Hoymiles** units (the HM and HMS series) are the most widely used micro-inverter family across plug-in-style kits sold in the US, largely because they're UL 1741-listed in multiple wattage tiers and pair with almost any standard 60-cell panel. Their app provides per-panel generation history, which is useful for the kind of DIY troubleshooting a plug-in owner without an installer has to do themselves.

**APsystems** (notably the EZ1 series) ships in a number of balcony and ground-kit bundles and is built specifically around the small-scale, DIY-installed use case rather than full rooftop arrays, with a simpler one-panel or two-panel setup than Hoymiles' modular string approach.

**Enphase IQ** micro-inverters are the gold standard for full rooftop installs, but they're overbuilt and overpriced for a single 400-800W plug-in panel — if you see Enphase hardware bundled into a budget plug-in kit, check the listing carefully, since this is a rarer and pricier combination than the market norm.

**Growatt** and a handful of newer entrants appear in budget kits, generally at a lower price point; verify their UL 1741 listing independently rather than taking a seller's word for it, since enforcement of labeling accuracy varies by marketplace.

## Pairing panels correctly

Most micro-inverters specify a maximum DC input voltage and current range, and pairing the wrong panel can either underperform the inverter's capacity or, in rarer cases, exceed its rated input. If you're buying panel and inverter separately rather than as a bundled kit, check both spec sheets before ordering — our [400W solar panel buying guide](/us/blog/best-400w-solar-panel-us-2026) covers panel-side specs that pair cleanly with the 600-800W inverter tier most plug-in setups use.

## Utility notification still applies

Even with a fully UL 1741-compliant micro-inverter, most utilities expect some form of interconnection notification for a grid-tied system, however small. Requirements vary significantly by state and provider — our [utility interconnection guide](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) covers what that process typically looks like, and it's worth doing before, not after, you plug a new kit in. ## The bottom line

The panel on a plug-in solar kit is largely interchangeable; the micro-inverter is where compliance, safety, and long-term reliability actually live. A UL 1741-listed unit from an established brand — Hoymiles and APsystems cover the large majority of the current US plug-in market — with per-panel app monitoring and an IP65 housing is the baseline worth insisting on, regardless of how good the panel specs look on paper.

### Can I replace a failed micro-inverter with a different brand?

Usually yes, as long as the replacement matches the panel's voltage and current output range and carries its own UL 1741 listing — but check your kit's warranty terms first, since some bundles void coverage if components are swapped.

### Do I need a separate micro-inverter per panel, or can one unit handle two panels?

Both exist on the market. Single-panel micro-inverters (like APsystems EZ1) are simpler to install and diagnose; dual-input units handle two panels per inverter at a slightly lower per-watt cost but make per-panel fault isolation harder.

### Is a higher wattage micro-inverter always better?

No — matching the inverter to your actual panel output and household use case matters more than maximizing capacity. An oversized inverter adds cost without added benefit if your panels can't feed it enough DC input to use that extra headroom.
