Maryland sits in an unusual spot for plug-in solar: it has one of the more supportive state net metering laws on the East Coast, but it's also split across several utility territories — BGE, Pepco, Delmarva Power, and Potomac Edison — each with its own interconnection quirks. If you're considering a small plug-in kit for a rowhouse in Baltimore, a suburban DC townhome, or a property on the Eastern Shore, the rules shift depending on which utility serves you.

## Where plug-in solar sits legally

Like the rest of the US, small plug-in solar kits (typically 200–800W) occupy a grey area under [NEC Article 705](/us/blog/nec-article-705-plug-in-solar-us). Maryland's Public Service Commission regulates net metering for formally interconnected systems above a certain size, but it has no specific statute addressing small plug-and-play panels that simply connect through a standard wall outlet. In practice, most Maryland households running a sub-800W kit with a [UL 1741-listed microinverter](/us/blog/ul-1741-vs-ieee-1547-plug-in-solar-us) do so without going through formal utility interconnection — the system is small enough that it doesn't register as a net export concern, and these kits are designed to self-consume rather than export to the grid.

If you want a fully interconnected, utility-sanctioned system, Maryland's net metering law requires approval through your specific utility before any formal export compensation applies — see our [net metering vs net billing guide](/us/blog/net-metering-vs-net-billing-plug-in-solar-us) for how that process generally works. For most plug-in kit buyers, that formal step isn't necessary because the system is sized to offset your own daytime load, not to export meaningfully.

## Utility territory differences

**BGE (Baltimore Gas and Electric)** — covers Baltimore City, Baltimore County, and much of central Maryland. BGE has a relatively modern smart meter rollout, which makes it easy to see your plug-in kit's impact on daily usage through their online portal.

**Pepco** — serves Montgomery and Prince George's Counties, the DC suburbs. Pepco's territory has some of the highest electricity rates in the state, which improves plug-in solar payback. Many Pepco customers live in townhomes and rowhouses where a small balcony or patio-mounted kit is the only realistic option — similar to the use case covered in our [apartment and balcony guide](/us/blog/balcony-solar-installation-guide-us).

**Delmarva Power** — serves the Eastern Shore. Lower housing density means more ground-mount opportunities, but also more salt-air exposure near the Chesapeake Bay — worth checking panel frame corrosion resistance.

**Potomac Edison** — serves western Maryland (Frederick, Hagerstown, and westward). More rural, generally lower rates than Pepco, so payback periods run slightly longer.

## HOA and condo considerations

Maryland has a state law (similar to many "solar rights" statutes) that limits HOAs from outright banning solar installations, but the protections are generally written around rooftop systems with permits, not portable plug-in kits on balconies or patios. If you're in an HOA or condo association, it's worth checking your specific bylaws — see our general [HOA rules for plug-in solar](/us/blog/hoa-rules-plug-in-solar-us) guide for the questions to ask before you buy.

## What to buy for Maryland conditions

Maryland gets a reasonable, though not exceptional, solar resource — roughly 4.5–5 peak sun hours per day in summer, less in winter. Humidity and summer thunderstorms mean weather resistance matters more than in drier climates.

- **UL 1741-listed microinverter** — non-negotiable for safety and insurance purposes
- **IP67-rated connectors** — Maryland's humid summers and occasional ice in winter both stress cheap connectors
- **400–800W kit** — enough to meaningfully offset AC-driven summer peak loads without needing formal interconnection
- **Tilt-adjustable mount** — Maryland's seasonal sun angle swings enough that a fixed mount leaves output on the table in winter

## Estimated payback

Maryland's residential rates run roughly 17–22¢/kWh depending on utility and season, with Pepco customers typically at the higher end. A 600W kit self-consuming through daytime hours can offset $150–$250/year depending on your usage pattern and how much of that generation lines up with when you're actually home drawing power. On a $500–$700 kit, that puts payback in the 2.5–4 year range — faster than the US average thanks to Pepco and BGE's above-average rates.

If you're deciding between a plug-in kit and a full rooftop system eligible for the [federal tax credit](/us/blog/federal-tax-credit-plug-in-solar), the plug-in route is the lower-commitment option — no permitting, no roof penetration, and no long-term contract with an installer. For context on how that decision plays out elsewhere on the East Coast, see our guides to [Virginia](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-virginia) and [New Jersey](/us/blog/plug-in-solar-new-jersey), both of which share some utility and climate similarities with Maryland.

## FAQ

### Do I need to notify BGE or Pepco before installing a plug-in solar kit?

For small kits under roughly 800W that aren't formally interconnected for export credit, most Maryland households don't go through a formal notification process — the system is designed to self-consume rather than export. If you want official net metering credit, you'll need to apply through your specific utility; see our [utility interconnection guide](/us/blog/utility-interconnection-plug-in-solar-us) for the general process.

### Is plug-in solar worth it in Maryland compared to other states?

Yes, relatively — Pepco and BGE rates are above the national average, which shortens payback. Maryland's solar resource is moderate (not as strong as the Southwest, but better than the Pacific Northwest), so a correctly sized and angled kit performs reasonably well across most of the state.

### What's the best kit size for a Maryland rowhouse or townhome?

Most rowhouse and townhome setups in Baltimore or the DC suburbs work best with a 400–600W kit mounted on a balcony rail, patio stand, or small ground frame — see our [best plug-in solar kits](/us/blog/best-plug-in-solar-kits-us) roundup for current options sized for limited outdoor space.
