If you live in a flat, a terraced house, or anywhere with a bedroom window a few feet from where you'd mount a plug-in solar kit, "is it noisy?" is a fair question to ask before you spend £600-£900. Nobody wants to find out after installation that their new panels come with a faint electrical whine that's just loud enough to notice at 11pm.

The short answer: most plug-in solar kits are close to silent in normal use, but not every component behaves the same way, and where you live changes how much a small amount of noise actually matters. Here's what's actually generating sound, how the main UK products compare, and how to avoid a noisy setup altogether.

## Where any noise actually comes from

A plug-in solar system has two components that could theoretically make noise: the micro-inverter and, if your kit includes one, the battery unit.

**Micro-inverters** like the [Hoymiles HMS-800](/blog/hoymiles-hms800-review-uk) and the APsystems EZ1-M are passively cooled by design. There's no fan — heat is dissipated through the aluminium casing via natural convection, the same principle as a heatsink on a computer chip. That means the inverter itself produces no mechanical noise. What you might hear, very occasionally and only if you put your ear close to the unit, is a faint high-frequency electrical hum from the internal transformer or switching components — the same kind of sound you'd get standing next to an outdoor security light transformer. From a metre or more away, in a garden or on a balcony, it's inaudible over ordinary background noise.

**Battery-inclusive kits**, such as the [EcoFlow STREAM](/blog/ecoflow-stream-review-uk) system, are a different story. The battery pack is essentially a small power station, and like most lithium battery units it uses an internal fan for thermal management — particularly when charging quickly on a sunny midday peak, or when it's under heavy inverter load feeding appliances in the evening. This fan noise is usually described by owners as a soft whir, comparable to a laptop fan under load, and it only runs when the unit needs active cooling — not continuously. On a cool day with modest generation, the fan may not activate at all.

## Noise comparison at a glance

| Component type | Typical noise behaviour | Where it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Passive micro-inverter (Hoymiles, APsystems) | Silent to near-silent; no fan | Negligible anywhere |
| Battery pack under active charging/discharge | Soft fan whir, intermittent | Balconies, bedrooms nearby |
| Mounting/vibration noise (loose brackets, wind) | Rattling in high wind if poorly fitted | Any exposed mount |
| Existing appliances (fridge, boiler) for comparison | Quieter than most household appliances | — |

Manufacturers rarely publish formal decibel ratings for micro-inverters, largely because they're engineered to be silent by default rather than "quiet" — there's no fan to spec in the first place. Where noise does show up in real installations, it's almost always a mounting or vibration issue rather than the electronics: a bracket that's slightly loose, a panel edge buzzing against a wall in strong wind, or a cable knocking against guttering.

## Why this matters more in some UK homes than others

For a detached house with a garden, inverter or battery noise is essentially a non-issue — by the time it reaches a window, it's lost in normal outdoor ambient sound. It's a different calculation for:

- **Balcony installations in flats** — see our [balcony solar guide](/blog/balcony-solar-uk-guide) — where the kit often sits within a metre or two of a living room or bedroom window, and building acoustics can carry small mechanical sounds indoors more than you'd expect.
- **Terraced houses**, where a mounting bracket fixed to a party wall can transmit low-level vibration into next door, even if it isn't loud from outside. This is worth bearing in mind alongside our guide on [avoiding neighbour disputes over shading](/blog/plug-in-solar-neighbour-disputes-shading-uk) — noise complaints follow a similar pattern to shading ones.
- **Flats with no garden**, covered in our [guide for flats without outdoor space](/blog/plug-in-solar-flats-no-garden-uk), where balcony or window-box mounting puts equipment closer to living space by necessity.
- **Rented accommodation**, where a landlord or neighbour raising a noise concern could complicate an otherwise straightforward installation — worth checking before you commit, per our [buying checklist](/blog/plug-in-solar-buying-checklist-uk).

## How to minimise any noise before you buy

1. **Choose a passive (fanless) micro-inverter setup if you're noise-sensitive.** A standalone Hoymiles or APsystems inverter paired with panels, without a battery, is the quietest configuration available — there's simply no fan in the system.
2. **If you want a battery, check the mounting distance from windows.** A STREAM-style battery unit is best positioned a metre or more from a bedroom window if possible, or against an outward-facing wall rather than directly beneath a window.
3. **Torque-check your brackets after the first windy week.** Most "noise" complaints traced back after installation turn out to be a slightly loose panel frame or bracket bolt, not the electronics. A five-minute check with a screwdriver after the first storm usually solves it.
4. **Ask before you buy.** Retailers like B&Q, Currys, Screwfix, Wickes, Lidl and Amazon list specs for most kits — if fan operation isn't mentioned, it's reasonable to ask the manufacturer's support line directly, especially for battery-inclusive kits.
5. **Compare kits with your specific situation in mind.** Our [best plug-in solar kit guide](/blog/best-plug-in-solar-kit-uk) and the [STREAM vs Anker SOLIX RS40P comparison](/blog/ecoflow-stream-vs-anker-solix-rs40p-uk-2026) both note battery and fan behaviour where it's relevant to the buying decision.

## What the regulations say

SI 2026 No. 848, the statutory instrument that made plug-in microgenerators legal from 27 August 2026, sets electrical and safety limits — the 800VA/3.5A output cap, the BS 1363 plug requirement, the 5A fuse, no battery in the plug circuit itself, no extension leads, and one unit per household. It doesn't set a noise limit, because passively-cooled micro-inverters within the legal spec simply don't produce enough sound to warrant one. If you're buying a compliant kit from a mainstream retailer, you're already buying into a category of product that was never expected to be audibly disruptive.

That said, general nuisance law still applies in the same way it would to any garden equipment or appliance — persistent, genuinely disruptive noise from a badly mounted unit could theoretically be a neighbour dispute matter, though in practice this is vanishingly rare with plug-in solar compared to, say, an air source heat pump or a pool pump.

## The bottom line

For the vast majority of UK buyers, plug-in solar is effectively silent: passively-cooled micro-inverters have no fan and produce no meaningful noise at any normal distance. The one exception is battery-inclusive kits like the EcoFlow STREAM, which may run a quiet internal fan during heavy charging or discharging — noticeable up close, but rarely a problem once you're back indoors with a window closed. If you're installing on a balcony a few feet from where you sleep, it's worth choosing your mounting position with that in mind, and checking your brackets are properly torqued after the first windy spell. ### Do plug-in solar panels make noise?

The panels themselves are completely silent — they have no moving parts. Any sound comes from the micro-inverter (which is usually fanless and near-silent) or, in battery-inclusive kits, from the battery's cooling fan during heavy use.

### Is the EcoFlow STREAM noisy?

The STREAM's micro-inverter is passively cooled and produces no fan noise. If your kit includes the battery unit, it may run a soft internal fan during fast charging or high discharge, comparable to a laptop fan under load — audible up close but not from a few metres away.

### Will my neighbours hear my plug-in solar kit?

In almost all cases, no. Passively-cooled inverters produce no audible sound beyond normal background noise, and even battery fan noise doesn't typically carry beyond a metre or two. Loose mounting brackets rattling in wind are a far more common source of noticeable noise than the electronics themselves.
