With Black Friday and the summer buying season approaching, a wave of portable power stations, panels and solar kits are about to land in Australian homes — and inevitably, a share of them will be faulty, misdescribed, or simply not fit for what the buyer actually wanted to do with them. Unlike some overseas markets where return rights depend heavily on individual retailer policy, Australia has a strong statutory backbone under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL) that applies regardless of what a retailer's own returns page says. Here's what that actually guarantees you when something goes wrong with a plug-in solar purchase.

## The consumer guarantees, in plain terms

The ACL attaches automatic consumer guarantees to almost everything sold in Australia, and they can't be excluded or watered down by a store's own warranty terms, a "no returns on sale items" sign, or a manufacturer's limited warranty booklet. For a plug-in solar kit, the relevant guarantees are that the product must be of acceptable quality (safe, durable, free from defects, and working as a reasonable person would expect), fit for any purpose you told the seller about before buying, and match its description — including any claims made about [CEC approval](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au) or [AS/NZS 4777 compliance](/au/blog/as-nzs-4777-plain-english-plug-in-solar-au) in the listing or packaging. If a retailer advertises a kit as CEC-listed and it isn't, that's a straightforward breach of the guarantee that the goods match their description, separate from any issue with the hardware itself.

## Major failure vs minor failure — why it matters

The ACL splits remedies into two tiers, and which one applies changes what you're entitled to. A minor failure — something a manufacturer can reasonably fix, like a faulty cable or a firmware bug — only entitles you to the seller's choice of a repair, replacement or refund. A major failure is more serious: the product doesn't work as it should and can't easily be fixed, it's unsafe, or it significantly differs from what was described (including an inverter marketed as grid-compliant for Australian use that in fact isn't [CEC-approved](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au)). For a major failure, you get to choose the remedy — a full refund or a replacement — not the retailer. This distinction matters a lot for plug-in solar specifically, because a kit that's been imported outside normal channels or misdescribed on compliance can easily cross from "minor annoyance" into "major failure" territory. ## The warranty doesn't change your ACL rights

A common tactic — not always malicious, sometimes just a sales assistant repeating what they were told — is pointing to a manufacturer's 12-month or 24-month warranty as if it's the full extent of your rights. It isn't. The [manufacturer's warranty](/au/blog/solar-panel-warranty-guide-australia) sits on top of your ACL guarantees, not in place of them, and a consumer guarantee claim can often be made well beyond the warranty period if the failure suggests the product wasn't of acceptable quality for how long a reasonable person would expect it to last. A $600 inverter that fails after fourteen months isn't automatically out of options just because the box said "12-month warranty."

## Who you claim against — and why that matters for imported kits

Your consumer guarantee claim is against the retailer you bought from, not the manufacturer, which is usually good news — it's generally easier to deal with an Australian retailer than chase an overseas manufacturer directly. This is one of the strongest arguments for buying through [established Australian retail channels](/au/blog/where-to-buy-plug-in-solar-kits-australia) rather than grey-market importers, even if the sticker price looks better from an overseas seller. If you bought directly from an overseas website with no Australian presence, your practical ability to enforce an ACL claim drops sharply, even though the legal right technically still exists.

## Where this intersects with the installation question

Faulty hardware claims under the ACL are separate from the compliance question of [whether grid-tied plug-in solar is legal to install yourself in Australia](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-diy-vs-licensed-electrician-australia) — a product can be perfectly ACL-compliant to sell while still requiring a licensed electrician and CEC-approved status to legally connect to your switchboard. Don't let a smooth refund process on a faulty unit distract from the separate question of whether the replacement unit is actually legal to install the way you're planning to use it; our [AS/NZS 4777 explainer](/au/blog/as-nzs-4777-plain-english-plug-in-solar-au) is the place to check that before you buy again.

### Can a retailer refuse a refund because the item was on sale?

No. The ACL consumer guarantees apply regardless of price or discount, and a "no refunds on sale items" sign has no legal effect against a genuine consumer guarantee claim for a faulty or misdescribed product.

### What if the retailer says I have to deal with the manufacturer instead?

You're entitled to make your claim against the retailer you bought from — they can't redirect you to the manufacturer as your only option. They may offer to coordinate with the manufacturer, but the legal obligation to remedy a consumer guarantee failure sits with the seller.

### Does the ACL cover a kit I bought directly from an overseas website?

The legal guarantees technically still apply if the seller is considered to be trading in Australia, but enforcement becomes far harder in practice with no local presence, no local contact, and potential jurisdiction issues. Buying through an Australian retailer is the safer path for anything you'd want real recourse on.
