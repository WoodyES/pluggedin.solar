Heat pump hot water systems are one of the most heavily rebated appliances in Australia right now, and for good reason — they use roughly a third of the energy of a standard electric resistance hot water system. A natural follow-up question for anyone who's installed one, or is planning to, is whether a portable plug-in solar setup can offset enough of that running cost to be worth adding. The honest answer: partially, and it depends heavily on when your unit actually draws power.

## How much power does a heat pump hot water system actually use?

Most residential heat pump hot water units in Australia draw somewhere between 800W and 2,400W while actively heating, cycling on and off rather than running continuously. Daily energy use for a typical household (3–4 people) is usually in the 2–4kWh/day range, noticeably less than the 6–10kWh/day a standard electric resistance system can use.

That daily draw is well within range of what a decent plug-in solar setup can meaningfully offset — but only if the timing lines up, which is the real issue.

## The timing problem

Most heat pump hot water systems are set to run on an off-peak timer, often overnight, to take advantage of cheaper tariffs. That's smart for minimizing cost on a flat or standard tariff, but it means the unit is drawing power exactly when your plug-in solar panels are producing nothing.

This is where [time-of-use tariffs](/au/blog/best-time-of-use-tariffs-australia-2026) matter. If you're on a tariff with a cheap daytime "solar sponge" window — as offered in [South Australia](/au/blog/solar-sponge-sa-tariff-plug-in-au) and [NSW](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-nsw-solar-sponge-au) — the economics actually favor shifting your hot water heating to run during the day, which is also exactly when your plug-in solar panels are producing. Many heat pump hot water controllers let you reprogram the timer to a daytime window instead of the default overnight setting — check your unit's manual or installer settings.

## Does a plug-in kit have enough capacity?

An 800W-class plug-in solar kit on a reasonable Australian rooftop or ground mount will typically produce somewhere in the 2.5–4.5kWh/day range depending on season and location — see our guides for [Queensland](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-queensland-heat-temperature-coefficient-au) and other states for region-specific numbers. That's broadly comparable to a heat pump hot water system's daily draw, which means in good conditions, a single kit could offset a meaningful share of your hot water heating cost — but it won't fully run the unit on its own, especially if the heat pump cycles at 1.5–2.4kW peak while your panel is only producing 800W peak.

In practice, most of the benefit comes from **reducing grid draw during the heating cycle**, not eliminating it — your panel output gets consumed first, and the heat pump pulls the rest from the grid as normal.

## What actually works in practice

1. **Switch the heat pump's timer to run during daylight hours**, ideally matching your panel's peak production window (typically late morning to mid-afternoon)
2. **Check whether your retailer offers a solar-sponge or time-of-use tariff** that rewards daytime use rather than penalizing it
3. **Don't expect full offset** — treat this as a partial reduction in hot water running costs, not a way to run hot water "for free"
4. **Monitor actual draw** with a [home energy monitor](/au/blog/best-energy-monitors-solar-australia) so you can see whether your panel output and heat pump cycles are actually overlapping, rather than guessing

## Is it worth the hassle for hot water alone?

If offsetting hot water heating is your only goal, a plug-in solar kit is a modest win at best — the timing mismatch between typical overnight heating and daytime generation limits how much you'll actually save unless you retime the heat pump's cycle. Where plug-in solar earns its keep is as a general household offset — covering a heat pump hot water system during the day when the timer allows, plus other daytime loads like [air conditioning](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-air-conditioning-australia), fridges, and general appliance use.

If you're deciding whether to add a kit specifically because of a heat pump hot water system, run the numbers with our [payback calculator](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-payback-calculator-australia) using your actual tariff and retimed usage pattern rather than assuming full offset.

## CEC compliance still applies

Whatever you're offsetting, the installation rules don't change. Grid-connected setups still need a [CEC-approved inverter under AS/NZS 4777](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au) and a licensed electrician for anything beyond a simple plug-in portable setup. If you're running the heat pump off a portable power station setup instead (common for off-grid or remote properties), see our [off-grid solar guide](/au/blog/off-grid-solar-australia-guide) for sizing a system that can handle the heat pump's peak draw, not just its average.

## Bottom line

A plug-in solar kit can meaningfully reduce the cost of running a heat pump hot water system, but only if you shift the heating cycle to overlap with daylight panel production — and even then, expect a partial offset rather than a full one. Check your tariff for a solar-sponge or time-of-use window, retime your heat pump if your retailer rewards daytime use, and measure actual results with a monitor rather than assuming the two schedules already line up.

### Should I run my heat pump hot water system during the day or overnight?

If you have plug-in solar panels and a flat or time-of-use tariff that doesn't penalize daytime use, running it during daylight hours lets your panel output offset the draw directly. If your tariff has a cheap overnight rate and no solar-sponge daytime window, overnight may still be cheaper overall even without solar offset — check your specific tariff before changing the timer.

### Will one plug-in solar kit fully power my heat pump hot water system?

Unlikely on its own. Most heat pump units draw more at peak (1.5–2.4kW) than a single 800W-class plug-in kit produces, so expect a partial offset during the heating cycle rather than the unit running entirely off solar.

### Do I need a licensed electrician to connect solar to my heat pump system specifically?

The hot water system itself doesn't change the rule — any grid-connected solar inverter still needs to be [CEC-approved under AS/NZS 4777](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au) and installed or signed off by a licensed electrician. A simple portable panel-to-power-station setup used off-grid doesn't trigger this requirement.
