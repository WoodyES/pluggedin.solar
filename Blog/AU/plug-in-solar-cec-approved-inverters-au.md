Anyone researching solar in Australia quickly runs into two acronyms: CEC (Clean Energy Council) and AS/NZS 4777, the standard governing grid connection of inverter-based generating systems. Both are central to why plug-in solar — the cheap, socket-connected kits that have taken off in the UK since August 2026 — remains in a genuine grey zone here rather than a straightforward legal product category. This article explains what the CEC approval system actually requires, and why it currently sits at odds with a true plug-and-play solar kit.

## What CEC approval actually covers

The Clean Energy Council maintains an approved products list for inverters, panels and batteries used in grid-connected systems. To qualify for any state or federal incentive — the STC (Small-scale Technology Certificate) scheme, state rebates, or feed-in tariff eligibility — a grid-tied installation generally needs to use CEC-approved equipment, installed by a CEC-accredited designer and installer. This isn't a paperwork formality; it's tied directly to safety compliance under [AS/NZS 4777](/au/blog/as-nzs-4777-plain-english-plug-in-solar-au), the standard covering how inverters must detect and respond to grid conditions, including anti-islanding protection equivalent to what UL 1741 requires in the US or G98 requires in the UK.

## Why plug-in kits don't fit neatly into this framework

A genuine plug-in solar kit — panel plus microinverter, connected via a standard household socket rather than wired into the switchboard by an electrician — sits outside the installation model CEC accreditation and AS/NZS 4777 compliance assume. The standard is written around fixed, professionally installed systems with a formal connection agreement to the local distribution network. A product plugged into a general power outlet doesn't go through that process, which is precisely why [plug-in solar remains legally unclear in Australia](/au/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-australia) even though the underlying microinverter technology (the same hardware used in UK and German kits) is often CEC-listed for its electrical safety characteristics.

## The practical distinction: grid-tied vs off-grid

This is where the framework actually matters for what you can buy today. A **grid-tied** system — one that exports even small amounts of power back through your home's wiring into the grid — falls under AS/NZS 4777 and needs a licensed electrician and, in most cases, network operator notification. A **portable, off-grid** setup — a solar panel charging a standalone battery or portable power station with no connection back to your home's electrical circuit — doesn't touch the grid at all, so the AS/NZS 4777 grid-interconnection requirements simply don't apply. That's why [off-grid and portable power station setups](/au/blog/off-grid-solar-australia-guide) are the practical, unambiguously legal route for most Australians right now, while UK-style plug-in kits that feed directly into household wiring remain a legal grey area here.

## What would need to change

For a genuine plug-in category to become legal in Australia the way it has in the UK, either AS/NZS 4777 would need a new compliance pathway specifically for low-power (typically sub-800W), socket-connected microgenerators — similar to how the UK's Interim Product Specification and [SI 2026 No. 848](/au/blog/what-europe-teaches-australia-about-plug-in-solar) carved out a distinct legal category for "plug-in microgenerators" separate from full rooftop solar — or individual states would need to issue specific exemptions. Standards Australia and the CEC have both been watching the UK and German markets closely, and [Victoria's 2026 apartment solar inquiry](/au/blog/victoria-apartment-solar-inquiry-2026) is widely seen as the most likely first mover if a formal pathway does emerge.

## What CEC-approved actually buys you today

If you do go down the licensed, grid-tied installation route — for example a small rooftop system on a house you own — insisting on CEC-approved inverters and CEC-accredited installers isn't optional if you want STC rebate eligibility, and it also matters for insurance: many home insurers require CEC-compliant installation for a solar claim to be honoured. For apartment and rental situations where a full accredited install isn't realistic, see our guides on [strata rules](/au/blog/strata-rules-solar-panels-australia) and [renting](/au/blog/solar-for-renters-australia) for what's actually achievable within the current framework.

### Does a plug-in solar kit need to be CEC-approved to be legal in Australia?

CEC approval specifically applies to grid-connected systems that qualify for rebates and STCs. A standalone, off-grid setup (panel charging a portable battery with no grid connection) doesn't need CEC approval to be used, since it never triggers the AS/NZS 4777 grid-interconnection requirements. A true grid-tied plug-in kit, however, would need to meet that standard, which is a large part of why the category remains unclear here.

### What's the difference between AS/NZS 4777 and CEC approval?

AS/NZS 4777 is the technical standard covering how grid-connected inverters must behave for safety (anti-islanding, voltage and frequency response). CEC approval is the Clean Energy Council's product listing and installer accreditation system built around that standard, used to determine rebate and STC eligibility. A product can technically meet AS/NZS 4777's electrical requirements without being on the CEC-approved list for rebate purposes.

### Can I legally use an off-grid portable solar and battery setup in Australia right now?

Yes. A portable solar panel charging a standalone battery or power station with no connection to your home's wiring or the grid doesn't require CEC approval, a licensed electrician, or network notification, because it never touches AS/NZS 4777's grid-interconnection scope. This is the most straightforward legal option for renters and apartment residents today.
