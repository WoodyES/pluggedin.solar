Most of the conversation around portable solar and rental housing in Australia focuses on the tenant's side: what a renter is allowed to plug in, and whether a landlord can say no. But a growing number of landlords are asking the opposite question — does it make sense to offer or install portable solar themselves, either as a tenant drawcard or as a way to cut outgoings on a property they still pay some running costs for? The answer depends heavily on the property type, the lease structure, and whether the building sits under strata.

## Why a landlord would bother

Unlike full rooftop solar, which only pays off for whoever holds the electricity account, a portable kit changes the calculation for a landlord in a few specific situations. Short-stay and holiday letting is the clearest case — see our [Airbnb hosts guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-airbnb-hosts-australia) for the detail — since the owner typically pays the electricity bill directly and keeps any savings. A rural or semi-rural rental where the landlord covers power for a shared pump, outbuilding or common area is another. And for standard long-term tenancies where the tenant pays their own electricity, offering a portable kit as an included amenity can be a genuine point of difference in a competitive rental market, even though the landlord won't see the energy savings directly.

## Strata properties: check the by-laws first

If the rental is a unit or townhouse within a strata scheme, the by-laws — not state tenancy law — are usually the binding constraint on what can be fixed to a balcony, wall or common-property area. Our [strata rules guide](/au/blog/strata-rules-solar-panels-australia) covers what's typically allowed without a vote and what needs owners' corporation approval. As a landlord, you have more standing to pursue a by-law exception or raise it at a general meeting than a tenant would, since you're the registered owner — but the outcome still depends on the scheme's specific rules, and a portable, non-fixed kit on a private balcony is almost always the path of least resistance regardless of who's asking.

## Compliance is the landlord's responsibility either way

Because grid-tied solar in Australia requires a CEC-approved inverter installed under [AS/NZS 4777](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au) by a licensed electrician, anything a landlord supplies as a fixture has to meet that standard regardless of who ends up using it. This is exactly why most landlords who go down this road choose a genuinely portable, plug-and-run setup rather than anything wired into the switchboard — it sidesteps the licensed-installer requirement entirely and keeps the equipment classed as a removable appliance rather than part of the property's fixed electrical installation. If you're supplying equipment as landlord rather than tenant-purchased, our [legality guide](/au/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-australia) sets out exactly where that line sits.

## Insurance and liability

A portable panel you supply as landlord is still your asset and your liability if it's not secured properly — a panel that blows off a balcony in a storm and causes damage or injury is a landlord's problem to the same extent a loose gutter or balustrade would be. Our [home insurance guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-home-insurance-australia) covers what to tell your landlord insurance policy before you supply equipment at a rental property, and it's worth doing before, not after, something goes wrong. If the property is let through a managing agent, loop them in too — most agency management agreements require any added fixtures or equipment to be disclosed and insured correctly.

## Tenant turnover and the numbers

The economics only work if the kit survives multiple tenancies in reasonable condition, since a portable setup that needs replacing every time a tenant moves out erases most of the benefit. Budget for a basic condition check at each change of tenancy — panels and cabling are fairly robust, but connectors and mounting hardware take the most wear. On the financial side, a landlord who pays electricity directly (short-stay lets, granny flats with shared metering) can expect a single [400W kit](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-vs-rooftop-solar-australia) to offset a modest but real slice of base-load consumption — enough to matter on a property with no existing solar, but not a substitute for full rooftop solar if you're planning to hold the property long-term and want a bigger return.

## Granny flats and secondary dwellings

If the rental in question is a granny flat or secondary dwelling on a larger block, see our dedicated [granny flat and secondary dwelling guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-granny-flat-secondary-dwelling-au) — these often have their own metering setup that changes what's practical, and landlords letting out a secondary dwelling while living in the main house have more flexibility to install something more permanent on their own side of the meter.

## Remote and regional rentals

Landlords with rural rentals, particularly anything off-grid or on tank and generator power, are a different case again — see the [off-grid and remote properties guide](/au/blog/off-grid-solar-remote-au-properties) for what's realistic there. A tenant relying on a generator for baseline power can benefit meaningfully from a landlord-supplied portable panel even where full off-grid solar isn't in the budget.

### Can a landlord install solar on a rental property without telling the tenant?

In most states, landlords need to give notice before entering the property or carrying out work, even minor work like mounting a bracket, under standard residential tenancy rules. Supplying a genuinely portable, plug-in kit that the tenant sets up themselves avoids most of this, but any landlord-initiated installation work still needs the usual entry notice.

### Does a portable solar kit need to be disclosed to the next tenant?

Yes — if it's included as part of the tenancy (an inclusion in the lease or a listed amenity), it should be noted in the condition report and lease documents the same as any other supplied appliance, both for insurance purposes and so the next tenant knows what they're inheriting.

### Is it worth a landlord installing full rooftop solar instead of a portable kit?

Only if the landlord pays the electricity bill directly or the property is held long-term with a clear financial case — for most standard tenancies where the tenant pays their own power, a portable kit is the more sensible scale of investment, since the landlord doesn't capture the ongoing savings from a full rooftop system.
