One of the genuine advantages a portable solar kit has over a full rooftop system is that it's yours to keep, not a fixture that transfers with the property title. If you're moving house — whether that's a rental, an owned home, or a granny flat — your kit should come with you. But "can I take it" and "should I take it, and how" are different questions, and the answer depends on how it was mounted, how it's wired, and whether anything needs disconnecting properly first.

## Ownership is rarely the issue

Because a compliant portable kit sits outside the fixed electrical installation covered by [AS/NZS 4777](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au) — it's a plug-and-run appliance, not a licensed-electrician install — it's legally treated as a removable item you own outright, the same as a dishwasher or a portable air conditioner you bought yourself. This is true whether you bought it as a homeowner or as a tenant; see our [legality guide](/au/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-australia) if you want the full compliance picture. The practical question at move time is almost always about the mounting and wiring, not ownership.

## If you're renting: check your lease and strata first

Tenants who installed a portable kit during a tenancy should revisit whatever arrangement let them install it in the first place — our [renters guide](/au/blog/solar-for-renters-australia) and [state-by-state renters guide](/au/blog/solar-for-renters-state-by-state-australia) cover what most leases actually require. If the property is under strata and you needed a by-law exception or owners' corporation sign-off to mount the panel on a balcony rail, check our [strata rules guide](/au/blog/strata-rules-solar-panels-australia) for whether that approval was tied to you personally or to the unit — it matters for whether the next tenant inherits any restriction, and for what you're obligated to leave or remove.

## Disconnect and inspect before you pack it

Unplug the inverter from the wall outlet first, then disconnect the panel from the inverter at the MC4 connectors rather than yanking cabling loose. Check connectors for corrosion or wear before you move — a kit that's been outdoors for a year or two, especially near the coast, can have connector degradation that's easy to miss until you try to reconnect it at the new place. If your kit uses a ground-mount frame or a balcony rail bracket, most hardware disassembles with basic tools; keep the fixings together with the panel so you're not hunting for replacement bolts when you set up again.

## Re-siting at the new property

Don't assume the new property's best spot matches the old one. A kit that lived on a north-facing Queensland balcony might need a completely different approach at a Victorian townhouse with a shaded courtyard — re-check orientation and shading before you remount, rather than defaulting to wherever the old brackets happened to fit. If the new place is a granny flat, secondary dwelling, or has its own separate metering, our [granny flat guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-granny-flat-secondary-dwelling-au) covers the specific wiring and metering quirks that come up there. And if the move takes you somewhere with a materially different climate — say, from temperate Victoria to sub-tropical Queensland — it's worth skimming the relevant [state guide](/au/blog/solar-guide-queensland) for anything state-specific around heat, humidity or storm exposure that didn't apply at the old address.

## Moving to acreage or an off-grid property

If the move is to a rural or remote property, a portable kit that was previously just a bill-trimming extra in suburbia can become genuinely useful as part of a broader off-grid setup. Our [off-grid remote properties guide](/au/blog/off-grid-solar-remote-au-properties) covers what changes when solar isn't a supplement to mains power but a meaningful part of your actual supply, and the [Grey Nomad camping guide](/au/blog/camping-caravan-plug-in-solar-grey-nomad-au) is worth a look too if the same kit is going to do double duty between the new property and a caravan.

## Selling versus renting out your old place

If you're not selling but renting out your previous home, decide upfront whether the kit stays as a tenant drawcard or comes with you — our [landlords guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-landlords-australia) covers the economics of leaving portable solar behind as an included amenity versus taking it to your new address. There's no single right answer; a kit that's paid for itself already is cheaper to leave than to professionally remount, while a newer kit is usually worth taking.

## Insurance continuity

Whatever home insurance arrangement covered the kit at the old address doesn't automatically transfer — notify your new insurer or update your policy once it's installed at the new property, the same as you would for any other owned appliance of meaningful value. Our [home insurance guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-home-insurance-australia) covers what most Australian insurers expect to be told about a portable solar setup.

### Can I legally take my portable solar kit when I move house in Australia?

Yes. A compliant portable kit is a removable appliance you own, not a fixture tied to the property, whether you installed it as an owner or a tenant with landlord permission.

### Do I need an electrician to disconnect or reinstall a portable solar kit when moving?

No, for a standard plug-and-run kit. Unplugging from the wall outlet and disconnecting panel-to-inverter MC4 connectors is a DIY task; you'd only need an electrician if the kit was ever wired into the property's fixed circuits, which falls outside the portable category entirely.

### Should I re-check compliance after moving to a new state?

It's worth a quick check. CEC-approved product status and AS/NZS 4777 compliance are national, but state-specific tariffs, climate exposure and any local strata or council rules can differ enough to be worth revisiting at the new address.
