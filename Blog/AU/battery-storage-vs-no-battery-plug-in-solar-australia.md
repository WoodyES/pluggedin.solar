Australia's plug-in and portable solar market sits outside the standard grid-tied framework — under AS/NZS 4777, a genuinely grid-interactive inverter requires CEC-approved equipment and a licensed electrician, which is why portable and off-grid setups have become the practical route for balcony, renter, and secondary-property use. Within that portable category, the single biggest cost decision is whether to add battery storage or run panels straight into what you're powering. Here's how to think about it.

## The case for no battery

A panel-only setup — just solar panels feeding a portable power station or directly into DC appliances during daylight — is the cheapest way into solar. You're paying for panels, cabling, and mounting hardware, and nothing else. For many use cases this is genuinely sufficient:

- **Daytime-only loads.** Pool pumps, irrigation timers, and workshop tools that you use during the day get power exactly when the panel is producing it. Our guide to [solar for pool pumps](/au/blog/solar-for-pool-pump-australia) covers this pattern in detail.
- **Grid-connected homes topping up daytime consumption.** If you're just trying to shave daytime grid draw at a rental or granny flat, a battery adds cost without adding much benefit — you're not trying to run anything after dark from this system.
- **Budget-constrained first setups.** A basic 400W panel-only kit costs a fraction of an equivalent kit with a decent LiFePO4 battery attached, since batteries are frequently the single most expensive component.

## The case for a battery

Batteries change what portable solar can do, and for specific use cases they're close to essential:

- **Off-grid and remote properties.** If there's no grid connection at all, storage isn't optional — without it you have power only while the sun is shining. Our [off-grid solar guide for remote AU properties](/au/blog/off-grid-solar-remote-au-properties) and broader [off-grid solar Australia guide](/au/blog/off-grid-solar-australia-guide) go through sizing a system properly for this scenario.
- **Grey Nomad and caravan setups.** Touring caravans and camper setups need power in the evening — for lighting, fridges, and devices — long after the panel has stopped producing. See our dedicated [camping and caravan grey nomad guide](/au/blog/camping-caravan-plug-in-solar-grey-nomad-au) for how battery sizing typically works for touring rigs.
- **Bushfire and blackout resilience.** A charged battery is available power during a grid outage, which a panel alone is not, since panel output stops the moment the sun does (and often well before, if smoke haze cuts generation). Our guide on [bushfire resilience and plug-in solar](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-bushfire-resilience-au) covers this angle specifically.
- **Solar Sponge and time-shifting tariffs.** In states running Solar Sponge-style tariffs — notably NSW and SA — cheap midday power is exactly when a panel-fed battery can charge for free, then discharge during the expensive evening peak. Our [NSW Solar Sponge guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-nsw-solar-sponge-au) and [SA Solar Sponge tariff guide](/au/blog/solar-sponge-sa-tariff-plug-in-au) explain how to actually capture this value.

## What it costs

Battery capacity is priced per kWh, and Australian retail prices for portable LiFePO4 batteries suitable for this kind of setup generally run from several hundred to well over a thousand dollars depending on capacity. Our [solar battery cost guide for Australia](/au/blog/solar-battery-cost-australia-2026) has current indicative pricing, and our [battery sizing guide](/au/blog/solar-battery-sizing-guide-australia) walks through matching capacity to your actual evening load rather than over- or under-buying.

As a rough rule of thumb: doubling your budget by adding a battery roughly doubles your system's usefulness only if you actually have loads that need power outside daylight hours. If everything you want to run only happens during the day, that spend is better put toward a larger panel array instead.

## CEC approval still matters

Whichever way you go, the inverter and any grid-interactive components should be CEC-approved products if there's any possibility of eventual grid connection, and compliant with AS/NZS 4777 if genuinely grid-tied. Our [plain-English AS/NZS 4777 guide](/au/blog/as-nzs-4777-plain-english-plug-in-solar-au) and [CEC-approved inverters guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au) both cover what to check before buying, since a non-compliant inverter can create both safety and insurance problems regardless of whether you added a battery.

## A simple decision framework

1. **Do you need power after sunset or during an outage?** If yes, get a battery.
2. **Is the system fully off-grid (no other power source at all)?** If yes, a battery is close to mandatory.
3. **Are you on or considering a Solar Sponge-style tariff?** If yes, a battery lets you actually use the cheap midday window.
4. **Is everything you're powering used only in daylight, on a grid-connected property?** If yes, skip the battery and put the budget into panels instead.

### Do I need a battery for a basic balcony solar setup in Australia?

Not necessarily. If you're only offsetting daytime grid consumption at a grid-connected home or apartment, a panel-only setup is usually the more cost-effective choice.

### How much does adding a battery typically add to the cost?

Often close to doubling the total system cost, since the battery is frequently the single most expensive component in a portable solar kit. Check our [battery cost guide](/au/blog/solar-battery-cost-australia-2026) for current indicative figures.

### Is a battery worth it just for blackout backup?

For bushfire-prone or outage-prone areas, many owners consider it worthwhile even without a Solar Sponge tariff or off-grid need, since it's the only way a portable system can supply power once the grid goes down.
