If you've priced up a full rooftop solar system in Australia, you've seen the STC discount applied automatically at the quote stage — it's usually the single biggest line item knocking down the upfront cost. So it's a reasonable question when you're looking at a portable plug-in kit instead: does the same rebate apply? The honest answer, for almost everyone reading this, is no — and understanding exactly why matters for getting your cost comparison right before you buy.

## What an STC actually is

Small-scale Technology Certificates are tradeable credits created under the federal Renewable Energy Target scheme. When a qualifying solar system is installed, it generates a number of STCs based on its size, location and the number of years left until the scheme phases down to zero in 2030. Installers typically take an upfront assignment of these certificates from the homeowner and apply their market value as a discount on the quoted price — which is why rooftop solar quotes look like they already include "the rebate" baked in, rather than you claiming anything separately afterwards.

## Why most plug-in kits miss out

STC eligibility isn't really about the panels or the inverter themselves — it's about the installation meeting the Clean Energy Regulator's requirements, which in practice means:

- The inverter must be **CEC-approved** and on the official product list
- The installation must be carried out (or signed off) by a **CEC-accredited designer and installer**
- The system must be connected in a way that satisfies **AS/NZS 4777**, the grid-connection safety standard

A portable, plug-into-a-wall-outlet kit — the kind covered throughout our [AU complete guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-australia-complete-guide) — is designed specifically to sit outside that licensed-installer framework. That's the whole appeal: no electrician, no paperwork, no permit wait. But it's also exactly why it doesn't generate STCs. You can't have it both ways under the current rules — the STC scheme and the "no licensed installer required" convenience of plug-in kits are, by design, mutually exclusive in Australia right now. For the compliance detail behind why, see our explainer on [CEC-approved inverters](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au).

## What this actually costs you in the comparison

Say a small rooftop system earns roughly $350–$600 in STC value depending on your state, zone and the scheme year — that's real money that shows up as a discount on a quoted installed price, not as something you'd have gotten in cash anyway for doing nothing. A plug-in kit at a comparable capacity doesn't get that discount, so the fair comparison isn't "kit price vs. rooftop quoted price" — it's "kit price vs. rooftop quoted price plus what the STCs were actually worth before the discount was applied." Run that comparison properly using our [solar panel cost guide](/au/blog/solar-panel-cost-australia-2026) before assuming a plug-in kit is automatically the cheaper path; for very small capacities it usually still is, but the gap is smaller than the sticker prices suggest.

## Are there any state rebates that do apply?

Some state-level programs are narrower and occasionally more flexible than the federal STC scheme, but almost all of them still require CEC-accredited installation to qualify — the same blocker as STCs. If you're specifically chasing rebate eligibility, a licensed grid-tied install is the only route; our guides to [NSW's Solar Sponge tariff](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-nsw-solar-sponge-au) and [South Australia's Solar Sponge tariff](/au/blog/solar-sponge-sa-tariff-plug-in-au) cover a different kind of saving — one that plug-in kits genuinely can access, because it's about *when* you use electricity rather than an installation rebate.

## When a licensed install actually wins the maths

If you own your home, have roof space, and were already planning to spend $4,000+ on a proper rooftop system, the STC discount is a genuine reason not to default straight to a portable kit just for simplicity. For renters, apartment dwellers, or anyone who can't get a licensed install approved — see our [renters' guide](/au/blog/solar-for-renters-australia) and [strata rules explainer](/au/blog/strata-rules-solar-panels-australia) — the STC comparison is moot anyway, since a licensed grid-tied install usually isn't on the table regardless of rebates.

## The bottom line

Don't go into a plug-in kit purchase expecting a rebate that isn't coming, and don't let a rooftop installer's STC-discounted headline price make a small plug-in kit look artificially worse than it is — compare the pre-discount rooftop price instead. Both numbers need adjusting before the comparison is fair.

### Can I claim STCs myself after buying a plug-in solar kit?

No. STC eligibility is tied to CEC-accredited installation at the time of connection, not something you can apply for retroactively on a self-installed portable kit, regardless of the equipment's quality.

### Will plug-in solar kits ever qualify for STCs under current rules?

Not under the scheme as it currently operates — STCs are structurally tied to licensed, CEC-compliant grid-tied installation. Any change would require the Clean Energy Regulator to create a new eligibility pathway specifically for portable or plug-in systems, which hasn't happened.

### Is a rooftop system definitely cheaper than a plug-in kit once STCs are factored in?

Not necessarily for small capacities — STCs reduce rooftop cost but don't eliminate the gap for a 1–2 panel equivalent system, and you still need roof access, ownership, and a licensed installer. For small, flexible or rental setups, a plug-in kit is usually still the practical choice despite missing the rebate.
