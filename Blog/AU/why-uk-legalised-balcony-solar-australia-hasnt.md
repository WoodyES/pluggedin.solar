Since 27 August 2026, anyone in the UK has been able to legally buy a plug-in solar kit, plug it into a normal wall socket, notify their network operator, and start generating — no electrician, no CEC-style accreditation, no grid-tied installation process. Australia, despite having similar sun, similar grid infrastructure quality, and a near-identical underlying microinverter technology, still hasn't created an equivalent pathway. We've covered [why that's historically been the case](/au/blog/why-australia-doesnt-allow-plug-in-solar-yet), but with the UK's law now two months into real-world operation, it's worth looking at exactly what the UK did differently — and whether any of it is realistically coming to Australia.

## What the UK actually built

The UK's approach, formalised in SI 2026 No. 848, creates an entirely new legal category called a "plug-in microgenerator" that sits outside the normal rooftop solar installation framework. The rules are narrow and specific by design: a maximum output of 800VA/3.5A, a standard domestic plug (BS 1363) into a dedicated socket on its own 5A fuse, no battery permitted in the plug circuit, and no extension leads. Crucially, the compliance burden sits with the hardware, not the installer — every legal kit must use a microinverter listed on the [Energy Networks Association's G98 compliant device register](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au), which tests for exactly the anti-islanding and grid-protection behaviour that [AS/NZS 4777](/au/blog/as-nzs-4777-plain-english-plug-in-solar-au) also demands of Australian inverters. The buyer's only remaining obligation is a straightforward DNO notification — not a licensed installation, not a signed-off commissioning certificate, just a notification that the device exists on their circuit.

## The key difference: who certifies safety, and when

This is the structural difference that actually matters, and it's the same one we flagged as the core sticking point in Australia's CEC framework. Australia's model certifies the *installation* — a licensed, CEC-accredited electrician designs, installs and signs off each system individually, every time, which is exactly why even a tiny balcony setup currently needs a tradesperson call-out here. The UK's model instead certifies the *product* once, centrally, through G98 testing, then lets any consumer install an already-certified device themselves, the same way you'd plug in a washing machine. Neither approach is objectively "safer" in the abstract — they're different points on the same safety-versus-accessibility tradeoff — but the UK's product-certification model is dramatically cheaper and faster to scale, which is a big part of why it's already produced dozens of compliant devices from multiple manufacturers within its first two months, tracked on our [live ENA compliant device register](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au).

## Two months of real-world results

It's still early, but the UK market hasn't shown the grid-stability problems regulators worried about. EcoFlow's STREAM range launched into UK retail in September 2026 and is now stocked across major retailers; multiple smaller manufacturers have added G98-certified kits to the register since. Household-level feedback has mostly centred on ordinary consumer issues — correct socket wiring, realistic generation expectations through a UK autumn — rather than any reported grid-protection failure. That two-month track record is exactly the kind of evidence Standards Australia and the Clean Energy Council would need to see before seriously considering an equivalent pathway here, and it's accumulating in real time.

## What would actually need to happen in Australia

A genuine Australian equivalent would need either a new, low-power compliance pathway written into or alongside AS/NZS 4777 — specifically for sub-800W, socket-connected devices, mirroring the UK's separate legal category rather than trying to fit plug-in kits into the existing grid-tied installation standard — or state-level exemptions covering specific use cases like apartment balconies. Neither is close to happening nationally, but [Victoria's 2026 apartment solar inquiry](/au/blog/importing-plug-in-solar-kits-australia) is the most concrete sign of movement, specifically because it's grappling with the apartment and renter access problem the UK's law was partly designed to solve. Germany got there first in Europe with a notification-based model closer in spirit to the UK's, and [what Europe's experience actually teaches Australia](/au/blog/what-europe-teaches-australia-about-plug-in-solar) is increasingly hard for regulators here to ignore as the UK data accumulates.

## What's legally available in Australia right now

None of this changes what you can legally do today. A grid-tied system of any size still needs a CEC-accredited installer and AS/NZS 4777 compliance — see our [plain-English AS/NZS 4777 guide](/au/blog/as-nzs-4777-plain-english-plug-in-solar-au) for what that actually involves. The genuinely legal, no-electrician alternative remains a standalone off-grid setup — a portable panel charging a battery or power station with zero connection to your home's wiring — which our [off-grid solar guide](/au/blog/off-grid-solar-australia-guide) covers in detail, and which is exactly what the affiliate card above points to. ### Could I legally import and use a UK-compliant plug-in solar kit in Australia?

Not for grid-tied use. A UK G98-certified kit still needs to meet AS/NZS 4777 and go through a CEC-accredited installation to be used as a grid-connected system in Australia — UK certification doesn't transfer. It could be used as a standalone, off-grid charging setup (panel to battery, no home wiring connection) without triggering that requirement, the same as any other portable solar product.

### Is Australia likely to adopt a UK-style plug-in solar law soon?

Not in the near term at a federal level. The more realistic path is a state-level pilot or exemption — Victoria's ongoing apartment solar inquiry is the clearest current candidate — rather than a nationwide change to AS/NZS 4777 itself.

### Why did the UK choose product certification instead of Australia's installer-certification model?

The UK was starting from a largely unregulated gap — plug-in kits weren't previously addressed by existing electrical installation law at all — so creating a new, narrowly-scoped product category was simpler than rewriting installation rules. Australia's AS/NZS 4777 framework is deeply embedded in how all grid-tied generation is regulated, which makes a parallel low-power pathway a bigger structural change to introduce.
