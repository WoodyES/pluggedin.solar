Australian Christmas lights have an advantage most of the world doesn't get: they go up right as the sun is at its strongest and the days are at their longest. A Melbourne or Sydney display running from early evening through to 10 or 11pm overlaps directly with the tail end of a long summer solar day, which makes this one of the few genuinely favourable matchups between a seasonal load and seasonal generation anywhere in the plug-in solar calendar. The question we get every November is whether a small portable kit can actually offset a serious lights display, or whether it's a marketing exercise. The honest answer is: partially, and the details matter.

## The legal and practical setup for Australia

Worth saying upfront because it trips people up every year: grid-tied solar in Australia requires a CEC-approved inverter installed under [AS/NZS 4777](/au/blog/as-nzs-4777-plain-english-plug-in-solar-au) by a licensed electrician — there's no equivalent to the UK's new plug-in microgenerator category here. That means a genuinely "plug into a wall socket and export to the grid" kit isn't the legal route in Australia the way it now is in Britain. For Christmas lights specifically, that's actually not a problem, because the practical setup isn't grid-tied anyway — it's a portable panel charging a battery, which then powers the lights directly as a standalone circuit, no grid interconnection involved at all. Our [CEC-approved inverters guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au) and general [is plug-in solar legal in Australia](/au/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-australia) explainer cover the distinction between grid-tied (needs the electrician and paperwork) and portable/off-grid (doesn't) in more depth.

## Running the actual numbers

A typical suburban Christmas lights display — a few hundred LED string lights, a couple of inflatable decorations with internal fans, maybe a projector — draws somewhere between 50W and 200W depending on how enthusiastic the display is. LED strings specifically are efficient; the bigger draws are usually fans in inflatables and any floodlighting rather than the string lights themselves.

- **A 200-400W portable panel** charging through the day can fully replenish a 1-2kWh battery well before lights-on time in December, given 12+ hours of strong summer daylight.
- **A modest 100W display** running 5 hours nightly uses about 0.5kWh — trivially covered by that stored charge with capacity to spare.
- **A heavier 200W display with inflatables** running the same 5 hours uses about 1kWh nightly — still comfortably within range of a decent battery fully charged from a day of December sun.

The economics aren't the point here — nobody's buying a solar kit purely to offset a few weeks of Christmas lights — but if you already own a portable panel and battery for camping or blackout prep (see our [grey nomad camping guide](/au/blog/camping-caravan-plug-in-solar-grey-nomad-au)), running your lights off it through December instead of the grid is close to free, and means your display isn't adding to your January power bill.

## Where state tariffs change the picture

If you're on a grid-tied home solar system already (the full CEC-approved rooftop kind, not a portable kit) rather than running lights off a standalone battery, your state's tariff structure matters a lot for December economics. NSW and SA both run [Solar Sponge-style tariffs](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-nsw-solar-sponge-au) with cheap daytime power — in SA specifically, see our [Solar Sponge SA breakdown](/au/blog/solar-sponge-sa-tariff-plug-in-au) — which means charging a battery during the cheap midday window and running lights off it in the evening peak can actually save money versus drawing straight from the grid at peak rates when your lights are on. That arbitrage angle works nicely alongside a Christmas lights display precisely because the usage window (early evening) lines up with peak pricing in most states.

## Don't forget the heat

December in most of Australia means real heat, and that affects two things worth planning around. First, panel efficiency drops somewhat at high cell temperatures — our [Queensland heat and temperature coefficient guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-queensland-heat-temperature-coefficient-au) explains why a 40°C day doesn't generate quite as well as the panel's rated spec suggests, though you're still getting strong output overall given the sheer number of daylight hours. Second, if your display and solar setup also need to survive storm season, cross-check our [summer blackout prep guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-summer-blackout-prep-australia) — a battery you're using nightly for lights is also one you could lean on if a summer storm takes the grid down, which is a nice bit of dual-purpose value from the same gear.

If you're buying new gear specifically for this, November is a sensible time to do it — ahead of the Christmas rush and before [Black Friday pricing](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-black-friday-deals-2026-au) potentially sweetens the deal anyway. Run your expected display wattage through our [payback calculator](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-payback-calculator-australia) if you want a clearer sense of whether a bigger kit pays for itself through the rest of the year, not just December.

### Do I need an electrician to run Christmas lights off solar in Australia?

Not for a standalone portable panel-and-battery setup powering lights directly — that's not grid-tied. You only need a CEC-approved inverter and licensed electrician for a system that connects to and exports into the grid under AS/NZS 4777.

### Can a small portable solar panel actually power a full Christmas lights display?

Usually yes for typical suburban displays (50-200W), given Australia's long December daylight hours. A 200-400W panel paired with a 1-2kWh battery comfortably covers several hours of evening lights with margin to spare.

### Does hot weather reduce solar panel output for December displays?

Slightly — panel efficiency dips a little at high cell temperatures — but the sheer length of summer daylight hours more than compensates, so overall daily generation in December is still strong despite the heat.
