Walk down almost any suburban street in Australia and you'll see them: freestanding carports and pergolas, usually with a flat or gently pitched colorbond roof, sitting unused for anything except shading a car or an outdoor table. For anyone running a portable or off-grid solar setup, that's one of the best mounting surfaces available — often better than the house roof itself, since it avoids the licensed-electrician and CEC-approval requirements that come with a grid-connected rooftop system.

## Why carports and pergolas work well for portable solar

Unlike a home's main roof, a freestanding carport or pergola isn't part of the switchboard-connected electrical installation, which is exactly why portable and off-grid setups sit outside the [AS/NZS 4777](/au/blog/as-nzs-4777-plain-english-plug-in-solar-au) framework that governs grid-tied inverters. As we cover in our guide to [why Australia doesn't allow plug-in solar yet](/au/blog/why-australia-doesnt-allow-plug-in-solar-yet), the practical, legal route for most Australian households is a portable or off-grid setup charging batteries directly rather than feeding into the home's wiring — and a carport roof is an ideal, already-elevated surface for exactly that kind of system.

Most carports face a reasonable angle for Australian conditions already, and colorbond steel roofing takes standard mounting rail clamps well. Pergolas vary more — a slatted or louvred pergola roof needs a different approach than a solid polycarbonate or colorbond one, discussed below.

## Solid colorbond or polycarbonate roofs

For a solid-roofed carport or pergola, the standard approach is the same rail-and-clamp system used on house roofs: aluminium mounting rails run across the purlins or battens, secured with roofing-grade screws through EPDM-sealed washers, with the panels clamped to the rails. This is the most secure option and the one best suited to [CEC-approved panels](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au) in the 200-400W range common in portable setups.

Check the roof's structural rating before adding panel weight, especially on an older pergola that wasn't engineered with extra load in mind — a standard rigid 400W panel weighs around 20-22kg, which is rarely an issue on a modern colorbond carport but is worth confirming on anything older or lighter-gauge.

## Slatted and louvred pergolas

A slatted timber or louvred aluminium pergola roof doesn't give you a continuous surface to clamp rails to, so the common workaround is a secondary mounting frame — a simple raised frame built across the top of the slats, either in aluminium angle or treated timber, that the panels then mount to as if it were a flat roof. This adds a small amount of extra cost and effort but avoids drilling individual slats, which weakens them and invites water ingress at each fixing point.

## Bushfire and wind considerations

Carports and pergolas are often more exposed to wind than a house roof, sitting lower and sometimes more open on the sides. In [bushfire-prone areas](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-bushfire-resilience-au), check your local council's BAL (Bushfire Attack Level) rating before mounting anything — some BAL ratings restrict combustible materials near the structure, which can affect mounting frame choice. For wind, follow the same spacing and edge-clearance guidance used for rooftop panels, and in cyclone-prone parts of [Queensland](/au/blog/solar-guide-queensland) and the Northern Territory, use fixings rated for the relevant wind region rather than generic hardware-store screws.

## Running the cable to your battery or power station

Once mounted, the cable run from a carport or pergola to a portable power station or battery bank is usually longer than a house-roof install, since carports often sit further from the house. Use outdoor-rated, UV-stable cable for any exposed run, and keep connectors off the ground and away from pooling water — the same principles covered in our [off-grid solar guide](/au/blog/off-grid-solar-australia-guide). If you're running the setup off a [portable power station](/au/blog/best-portable-power-stations-australia) rather than a fixed battery bank, a short, accessible cable run also makes it easy to bring the power station indoors overnight or during severe weather.

## Sheds and workshops as an alternative

If you don't have a carport or pergola but do have a [shed or workshop](/au/blog/solar-for-shed-workshop-australia), the same rail-mounting approach applies there too, often with an even simpler cable run straight into the shed's own power setup. Renters and those in granny flats without a suitable roof of their own should also check our [granny flat and secondary dwelling guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-granny-flat-secondary-dwelling-au) for mounting options that don't require landlord sign-off on the main house.

### Do I need council approval to mount solar panels on my carport?

Generally no for a portable, non-grid-connected setup — but check your local council's rules if your carport is in a heritage overlay, bushfire-prone area, or if the structure itself required a building permit, since some of those approvals carry conditions on later additions.

### Can I mount panels on a rented property's carport?

Only with landlord permission, and ideally in writing. A rail-mounted system involves roof penetrations, so treat it the same as any other modification requiring consent — see our [renters' guide](/au/blog/solar-for-renters-australia) for lower-impact alternatives if permission isn't available.

### Is a carport or pergola better than roof-mounting on the house?

For a portable setup, often yes — it avoids running cable through the house, keeps the system independent of the main electrical installation, and is usually easier to access for cleaning or storm preparation than a two-storey roof.
