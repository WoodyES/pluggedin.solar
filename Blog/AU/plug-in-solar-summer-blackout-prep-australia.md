## Why Summer Is Blackout Season in Australia

Spring is the time to get ready, because by the time Australia's peak demand season actually arrives — typically late December through February — it's too late to be shopping around. Heatwaves push air-conditioning load to its annual peak right as ageing network infrastructure is under the most strain, and AEMO has issued demand warnings for parts of the National Electricity Market in recent summers as reserve margins tighten on the hottest days. Add in the risk of storm and bushfire-related network damage on the east coast, and most Australian households that have been through a multi-hour summer blackout already know it's worth preparing for the next one before the heat actually hits.

Portable solar and battery kits have become one of the more practical ways households prepare, precisely because they sit outside the grid-tied framework that makes rooftop solar a slow, licensed-electrician process. You don't need a [CEC-approved inverter](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au) or AS/NZS 4777 compliance to run a portable panel into a battery power station — that framework only applies to systems that connect to and potentially export onto the grid. A standalone panel-and-battery setup charges during the day and discharges through an outage independently of the grid entirely, which is exactly what you want when the grid itself is the problem.

## What a Blackout-Prep Kit Actually Needs to Run

The realistic goal for most households isn't keeping the whole house powered — it's keeping the essentials running for a multi-hour or overnight outage: a fridge or freezer, phone and laptop charging, fans or a small evaporative cooler, medical equipment like a CPAP machine, and enough lighting to get through the evening safely. A 1–2kWh portable power station paired with a 200–400W folding or portable panel will typically cover that load list for 12–24 hours before needing meaningful daytime recharge, and recharges fully across a single sunny day even on a modest panel. ## Pre-Charging With Solar Sponge Tariffs

If you're on a time-of-use plan, charging your blackout-prep battery during the cheap midday window costs almost nothing, and in some cases you're effectively being paid to do it. NSW and SA both run "[Solar Sponge](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-nsw-solar-sponge-au)" style tariffs with very low or near-zero midday rates designed to soak up rooftop solar oversupply, and topping up a portable battery during that window is one of the cheapest ways to keep it blackout-ready without touching your grid-tied system at all. Our [NSW guide](/au/blog/solar-guide-nsw) and [Queensland guide](/au/blog/solar-guide-queensland) cover the state-by-state tariff differences in more detail.

## Heat Affects Both the Grid and Your Panels

The same heatwaves that drive blackout risk also reduce how efficiently solar panels themselves perform — most panels lose output as cell temperature climbs above about 25°C, which matters most in Queensland, inland NSW and South Australia. Our [Queensland heat and temperature coefficient guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-queensland-heat-temperature-coefficient-au) explains the effect in more detail, but the practical takeaway for blackout prep is simple: charge your battery in the morning or late afternoon where possible, rather than assuming midday sun automatically means peak charging performance.

For regional and bushland-adjacent properties, the blackout risk isn't just heatwave demand — it's the very real possibility of network damage from bushfire or storm activity cutting supply for days rather than hours. ## Don't Wait Until December

Spring — right now, through October and November — is genuinely the best window to sort this out. Retailers aren't yet in peak-season shortage, delivery times are normal, and you've got a couple of months to test your setup on an ordinary outage-free day before you actually need it. Run a test: switch the fridge and a few essentials onto the power station for an evening, confirm the panel recharges it fully the next day, and you'll know exactly what you're relying on before a real heatwave blackout puts it to the test.

### Do I need a CEC-approved inverter for a blackout-prep solar kit?

No. CEC-approved inverters under AS/NZS 4777 are required for grid-connected systems that can export power to the network. A standalone portable panel charging a battery power station, with no grid connection at all, sits outside that framework entirely.

### How much battery capacity do I need to get through a typical summer blackout?

Most Australian blackouts from heatwave demand or storms resolve within several hours to a day. A 1–2kWh power station comfortably covers a fridge, lighting, fans and device charging for 12–24 hours, while households with essential medical equipment or longer regional outage risk should size up toward 3kWh or more — see our [best batteries for blackouts guide](/au/blog/best-solar-batteries-blackouts-australia) for sizing by household.

### Will my portable solar panel still charge the battery on a smoky or overcast bushfire day?

Output drops meaningfully under heavy smoke haze or thick cloud — often by 50% or more — but a folding panel will still produce some charge on all but the darkest days. It's worth having your battery topped up in advance of known fire-risk days rather than relying solely on same-day charging.
