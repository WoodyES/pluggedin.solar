Every Australian summer brings the same conversation in storm-prone and bushfire-affected regions: is it finally time to buy backup power for the next blackout? The traditional answer has been a petrol or diesel generator, and for decades that was really the only practical option outside a full battery storage system tied to the grid. Portable solar and battery kits have changed that calculation, but they're not a straight upgrade in every respect — each approach has real trade-offs worth understanding before you spend the money, especially heading into another peak demand season where [summer blackout prep](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-summer-blackout-prep-australia) is front of mind for a lot of households.

## The case for a generator

Petrol and diesel generators remain unmatched on raw, sustained output. A mid-size petrol generator can produce 2-6kW continuously for as long as you keep feeding it fuel, which comfortably runs a fridge, air conditioning, power tools and lighting simultaneously — a load profile that a portable solar and battery setup generally can't match without a much larger (and far more expensive) battery bank. Generators are also immune to weather in a way solar isn't: a generator runs at 2am in the middle of a multi-day storm with zero sun just as well as it runs at midday, which matters if the blackout itself is caused by the kind of weather event that also knocks out solar charging for days at a time.

Upfront cost favours generators too, at least at the lower end — a basic 2-3kW petrol generator is often cheaper than a solar-and-battery kit with comparable peak output, particularly before you factor in panels.

## The case for solar and battery

Where portable solar genuinely wins is total cost over time, noise, and safety in enclosed or built-up settings. A generator needs fuel — petrol doesn't store indefinitely, needs rotating or stabilising, and during a genuinely widespread outage (the kind that follows a major storm or bushfire event) servo queues and fuel shortages are common exactly when you need fuel most. A solar and battery kit's "fuel" is sunlight, which doesn't run out, doesn't need storing, and recharges the system for free every day the outage continues. Over a multi-day outage, that's the difference between rationing a jerry can and simply waiting for tomorrow's sun.

Noise and fumes are the other major factor. Generators are loud enough to annoy neighbours in suburban settings and produce carbon monoxide that makes them genuinely dangerous to run indoors, in a garage, or close to windows — a real risk during storms when people are tempted to shelter equipment from rain. A portable power station charged by a solar panel produces neither noise nor emissions, which matters in apartment blocks, [strata properties](/au/blog/strata-rules-solar-panels-australia) and anywhere running a generator would draw complaints or breach body corporate rules.

## What each one is actually good at

In practice, the two options suit different blackout profiles rather than one simply beating the other:

- **A generator suits:** sustained high loads (air conditioning, power tools, a full fridge-freezer plus other appliances at once), multi-day outages with no sun at all, and rural or large properties where noise is less of a concern. Our [off-grid remote properties guide](/au/blog/off-grid-solar-remote-au-properties) covers sizing for larger, sustained loads.
- **A solar and battery kit suits:** essential-only loads (fridge, phone charging, lights, a CPAP machine, fans), suburban and apartment settings where noise matters, and outages where some sun is still getting through — which describes the majority of Australian blackouts, since most are caused by network faults or localised storm damage rather than the kind of multi-day total washout that defeats solar charging entirely.

A reasonable middle ground a lot of households land on: a solar and battery kit for the common case (a multi-hour to overnight outage with normal weather either side of it), with a generator reserved as a fallback for genuinely extended, no-sun events. You don't necessarily need to choose only one.

## Running cost over a typical outage

For a realistic overnight-to-24-hour outage, which covers the large majority of Australian blackouts, a petrol generator running intermittently might burn 5-10 litres of fuel — a real but modest cost per event. A solar and battery kit costs nothing to run beyond the upfront purchase, assuming there's enough daylight to recharge between uses. Where the generator's running cost adds up is frequency and duration: a household that loses power regularly, or lives somewhere prone to multi-day storm events, will spend meaningfully more on fuel over a year than the equivalent solar setup costs to simply own.

## Sizing either option correctly

Whichever route you choose, size to your actual essential load rather than trying to power the whole house. Our [fridge and freezer power guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-fridge-freezer-australia) and [best batteries for blackouts guide](/au/blog/best-solar-batteries-blackouts-australia) both break down realistic sizing by appliance, which applies whether you're sizing a battery bank or deciding what a generator actually needs to cover.

### Is a solar and battery kit a full replacement for a generator in Australia?

Not for every situation. It comfortably covers essential loads (fridge, lighting, device charging, medical equipment) through most common outages, but a generator still wins for sustained high loads like air conditioning or power tools, and for multi-day outages with little usable sun.

### Is it safe to run a petrol generator during a storm?

Only outdoors, away from windows and doors, and never in a garage or enclosed space — petrol and diesel generators produce carbon monoxide that can be lethal indoors. A solar and battery power station avoids this risk entirely since it has no combustion engine.

### Which option is cheaper over time?

Solar and battery kits cost nothing to run once purchased, since sunlight is free, while a generator's fuel cost adds up with every use. For households that experience blackouts regularly, solar is usually cheaper over a year or two despite often costing more upfront.
