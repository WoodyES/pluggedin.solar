Most guides to portable and off-grid solar in Australia focus entirely on the hardware — panel wattage, battery capacity, CEC approval. Fewer mention that the retailer sitting on your account can swing your actual savings by hundreds of dollars a year, independent of anything about the kit itself. If you're about to buy a portable power station or an off-grid setup and you haven't checked your retail plan in the last twelve months, there's a good chance you're about to under-deliver on the payback math before you've even opened the box.

## Why the retailer matters as much as the kit

A plug-in or portable solar setup's financial case comes down to one thing: how much of a dollar it saves you per kWh of grid electricity it displaces. If you're on a flat retail plan at 28c/kWh, every kWh your kit covers is worth 28 cents. If you're on a time-of-use plan where afternoon rates spike well above that, the same kWh covered during a peak window can be worth considerably more — and the same logic can work against you if your plan's off-peak rate is so cheap that daytime self-generation barely beats it. Comparing retailers and plan types before buying, not after, is the difference between a kit that pays back in three years and one that pays back in five on the exact same hardware.

## Solar Sponge and time-of-use plans change the calculation entirely

NSW and South Australia's [Solar Sponge tariffs](/au/blog/solar-sponge-sa-tariff-plug-in-au) deliberately make midday electricity cheap to soak up excess rooftop solar on the grid, which sounds like bad news for anyone relying on a portable kit for daytime self-consumption — midday power is already cheap, so displacing it saves less. But the same plans typically push rates up sharply in the evening peak, and that's exactly where a portable power station charged during the cheap midday window and discharged in the evening earns its keep. Our [NSW Solar Sponge guide](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-nsw-solar-sponge-au) and [best time-of-use tariffs guide](/au/blog/best-time-of-use-tariffs-australia-2026) go through this in more detail, but the short version: if you're on a Solar Sponge-style plan, a battery-equipped portable kit that shifts generation into the evening peak will usually out-earn one that only runs loads in real time during the day.

## Feed-in tariffs barely matter for portable kits — and that's a good thing

A lot of Australians assume a solar setup's payback depends heavily on the [feed-in tariff](/au/blog/solar-feed-in-tariff-australia-2026) their retailer pays for excess export. For a full rooftop system, that's true. For a portable power station or an off-grid setup, it's mostly irrelevant, because these systems are designed around self-consumption and storage, not export — there's nothing to export in most configurations, and the kits that could theoretically grid-tie through AS/NZS 4777 in Australia require a [CEC-approved inverter](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-cec-approved-inverters-au) and licensed electrician rather than a plug-and-play setup. That actually simplifies the retailer comparison: you're shopping on usage rates and time-of-use structure, not feed-in tariff tables, which narrows what you need to check down to one page of any retailer's plan comparison site.

## What to actually check before you buy

Pull up your last three bills and work out your average daytime usage rate, your peak-window rate if you're on a time-of-use plan, and whether your retailer offers a specific solar or storage-friendly plan — several now do, built around exactly the self-consumption-and-shift pattern a portable power station enables. If your current plan has a high daily supply charge relative to usage charges, a portable kit's savings will look smaller as a percentage of your total bill even if the per-kWh savings are solid, which is worth knowing going in rather than being disappointed by your first post-purchase bill. [Virtual power plant](/au/blog/plug-in-solar-virtual-power-plants-australia) programs are another avenue worth checking in on, particularly in states running [community battery](/au/blog/community-solar-australia-guide) and VPP trials that pay for flexible household battery capacity — some portable power stations qualify, though terms vary a lot by program and state.

## Don't let the retailer check delay the purchase

None of this is a reason to hold off buying a kit while you shop retailers for months — portable power stations and off-grid panels pay for themselves on almost any plan, just at different speeds. It's simply worth five minutes with your last few bills and a plan comparison site before you commit to a specific setup size, because a battery-equipped kit aimed at evening peak shifting is a meaningfully different purchase decision from one aimed purely at real-time daytime offset, and the right call depends entirely on what your retailer is charging you by time of day.

### Does my feed-in tariff affect how much a portable power station saves me?

Usually not much, because most portable kits don't export to the grid at all — they're built around self-consumption and storage. Your usage rate and time-of-use structure matter far more than your feed-in tariff for this category of product.

### Is it worth switching retailers before buying a plug-in solar kit?

It's worth checking, not necessarily switching immediately. If your current plan has a strong time-of-use structure with a cheap midday rate and expensive evening peak, a battery-equipped kit that shifts generation into the evening can out-earn a simple real-time setup, which affects which product you should buy, not just which retailer you're on.

### Do Solar Sponge tariffs make portable solar less worthwhile?

Not really — they change which product makes sense. Cheap midday power reduces the value of real-time daytime self-consumption, but it increases the value of storing that cheap power and using it during the evening peak, which is exactly what a battery-equipped portable kit does.
