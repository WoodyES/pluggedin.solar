If you've read about [BS 7671 Amendment 4](/blog/bs7671-amendment-4-explained) or the [G98 DNO notification process](/blog/g98-dno-notification-plug-in-solar), you might assume you've covered every regulatory base before plugging in a solar kit. There's one more piece that trips people up: **Part P of the Building Regulations**, the rule that decides whether electrical work in your home needs to be notified to your local council or carried out by a registered competent person.

It's a genuinely confusing area because plug-in solar sits in an unusual position — it's electrical, it's new, and it doesn't fit neatly into the examples Part P was originally written around. Here's how it actually applies.

## What Part P actually covers

Part P applies to electrical installation work in dwellings in England and Wales (Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own equivalent frameworks). It was introduced to catch unsafe DIY electrical work — rewiring a consumer unit, adding new circuits, running cables to a garden building — and make sure it's either done by a registered electrician or signed off by building control.

The key word is **installation work**. Part P is about fixed wiring: new circuits, altered circuits, consumer unit changes, additions to the fixed electrical system of the house. It was never written with a plug-in appliance in mind, because historically nothing that generated electricity was something you simply plugged into an existing socket.

## Where plug-in solar actually sits

This is the part that matters: a compliant plug-in solar kit, installed exactly as intended under [SI 2026 No. 848](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk), connects via a standard BS 1363 plug into an existing wall socket. You are not altering the fixed wiring of the property. You're not adding a new circuit, moving a consumer unit, or running new cable through walls. You're plugging an appliance — albeit one that generates power rather than consumes it — into a socket that's already there.

On that basis, a standard plug-in solar installation, done within the legal specification (800VA/3.5A max, BS 1363 plug, no extension leads, no battery in the plug circuit), generally falls **outside** Part P's notifiable work categories, in the same way plugging in a washing machine or an electric heater doesn't require council notification. The compliance burden instead sits with [BS 7671 Amendment 4](/blog/bs7671-amendment-4-explained) (the wiring regulation framework) and the product itself being tested against the relevant standard — not with Part P.

Where it gets more complicated is if your installation goes beyond a simple plug-and-play setup. If you're having an electrician add a dedicated outdoor socket specifically for your panel, running new cable to a garden office, or making any other change to the fixed wiring to accommodate the system, **that work is notifiable** under Part P regardless of what it's being installed for. The solar kit itself doesn't trigger Part P — but the electrical work you do around it might.

## The practical test to apply

A useful way to think about it: ask whether you're changing anything permanent about the house's electrics, or just using what's already there. Plugging a compliant kit into an existing outdoor socket — no. Getting an electrician to install a brand new RCD-protected outdoor socket circuit so you have somewhere safe to plug it in — yes, that part is notifiable, and it's worth checking our [installation guide](/blog/how-to-install-plug-in-solar-uk) before deciding whether you need that extra step at all.

This also means DIY is genuinely on the table for a straightforward plug-in installation in a way it wouldn't be for rooftop solar, which always involves new wiring, an isolator, and consumer unit work that squarely falls under notifiable Part P territory. That's one of the structural reasons plug-in solar was designed the way it was — the legal framework deliberately avoids triggering the heavier compliance route that full rooftop systems require.

## Why this matters for leaseholders and renters

If you live in a leasehold flat or you're renting, Part P isn't really the hurdle anyway — [leasehold permission](/blog/leasehold-flat-solar-permission-uk) or your [landlord's sign-off](/blog/plug-in-solar-landlords-uk) is the more relevant gate. But it's worth being able to tell a freeholder or landlord confidently that a compliant plug-in kit, used as intended, doesn't trigger building control involvement — it often removes one of the objections people raise before they've actually looked into it.

## What about MCS and insurance?

Part P sits separately from [MCS certification](/blog/mcs-certification-small-solar-systems-uk), which covers eligibility for scheme payments rather than building regulations, and separately again from notifying your [home insurer](/blog/plug-in-solar-and-home-insurance-uk) that you've added the kit — something worth doing regardless of whether Part P applies, since insurers care about what's connected to your property, not just whether council building control was involved. Always check your specific kit is on the [ENA compliant device registry](/blog/uk-compliant-plug-in-solar-devices-live) before buying, since Part P's lighter touch only applies to kits actually meeting the legal specification — anything outside that spec is a different conversation entirely.

## The bottom line

For the overwhelming majority of plug-in solar buyers — someone plugging a compliant kit into an existing socket with no new wiring — Part P simply isn't in play. It only becomes relevant if you (or your electrician) are doing additional fixed electrical work to support the installation, in which case that specific work needs to follow the normal notification route, same as any other home electrical job.

### Do I need to notify my council before installing plug-in solar?

No, not for a standard installation using an existing socket and a compliant kit within the legal spec. Part P covers changes to fixed wiring, not plugging an appliance into a socket that's already there.

### What if my electrician wants to add a new outdoor socket for my panel?

That new socket circuit is notifiable work under Part P, separate from the solar kit itself. Your electrician should handle the notification or be a registered competent person who can self-certify it.

### Does Part P compliance replace G98 DNO notification?

No — they're unrelated. Part P is about building regulations for fixed wiring; [G98 notification](/blog/g98-dno-notification-plug-in-solar) is about telling your electricity network operator you've connected a generating device. Most compliant plug-in installations need the latter but not the former.
