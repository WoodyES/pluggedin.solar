With [plug-in solar becoming legal](/blog/plug-in-solar-legal-27-august-2026-uk) on 27 August 2026, a question has started showing up in allotment Facebook groups and forums: can I just plug one of these new kits into my shed and run lights, a kettle, or a water pump? The honest answer for the vast majority of the UK's 300,000-plus allotment plots is no — but not for the reason most people expect, and there's a straightforward alternative that works today.

## Why the legal "plug-in microgenerator" category doesn't fit allotments

The new rules under [SI 2026 No. 848](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk) define a compliant kit very specifically: it has to plug into a standard BS 1363 socket that's part of a domestic property's fused wiring, capped at 800VA / 3.5A, with no extension leads and no battery in the plug circuit. The [interim product specification](/blog/plug-in-solar-interim-product-specification-uk) is built entirely around the assumption that the socket you're plugging into sits on a house's ring main, protected by consumer unit fuses and earthed to the property.

An allotment shed almost never has that. The overwhelming majority of UK allotment sites have no mains electricity supply at all — councils and allotment associations generally don't run power to individual plots, partly on cost grounds and partly because permanent electrical installations on rented plots raise liability and safety questions they'd rather avoid. Without a domestic socket to plug into, there's no legal plug-in microgenerator installation to speak of. This is the same limitation covered in our guide to [off-grid cabins and outbuildings](/blog/plug-in-solar-off-grid-cabins-uk) — the 800VA plug-in category is a grid-tied product, and it simply doesn't apply where there's no grid connection to tie into.

## Check your tenancy agreement before assuming anything

Even on the rare site that does have mains power to a communal building, most allotment tenancy agreements explicitly prohibit tenants from running permanent electrical installations, extending wiring from a shared supply, or making any structural alteration without written consent from the site association or council. Some sites ban mains electricity on individual plots outright, largely for fire and safety reasons given how many allotment sheds are built from ageing timber with no professional wiring inspection. Before spending any money, read your tenancy agreement or ask your allotment secretary directly — this is a site-by-site rule, not a national one, and getting it wrong risks losing your plot.

## The alternative that actually works: portable, battery-based, and unregulated

The good news is that the thing most allotment holders actually want — some power for a radio, phone charging, LED lighting in the shed, or running a small water pump — doesn't need a grid-tied plug-in microgenerator at all. A portable power station charged directly by its own solar panel sits completely outside the SI 2026/848 framework, because it never connects to a domestic electrical circuit. No [G98 DNO notification](/blog/g98-plug-in-solar-uk) is required, the [no-battery-in-the-plug-circuit rule](/blog/plug-in-solar-no-battery-rule-uk-explained) doesn't apply because there's no plug circuit, and the 800VA output cap is irrelevant because you're not feeding anything back into the grid.

This is the same approach covered in our guide to [powering a shed with solar](/blog/plug-in-solar-for-shed-uk) and the one most [caravan and motorhome](/blog/plug-in-solar-caravans-motorhomes-uk) owners already use: panel charges battery, battery runs your appliances directly, nothing touches mains wiring.

## Sizing a system for realistic allotment use

Most plot holders aren't trying to run a fridge freezer — it's lighting, charging, and the occasional pump. A rough daily budget looks like this:

- LED shed lighting (2 bulbs, 5W each, 2 hours) — 20Wh
- Phone and radio charging — 30Wh
- Small 12V water pump (used briefly, not continuously) — 100Wh
- **Total: roughly 150Wh on a typical visit**

A compact portable power station in the 1–2kWh range paired with a single foldable panel comfortably covers that with days of buffer between visits, since you're not drawing power continuously the way a house circuit does. If you want something bigger — running power tools for a build day, for instance — see our guide to [portable power stations for plug-in solar setups](/blog/best-portable-power-stations-plug-in-solar-uk) for options that scale up.

## Security is the real design constraint

Sheds on allotments get broken into more often than most homeowners realise, and a solar panel bolted to a roof is an obvious target. Most experienced plot holders take the power station home between visits and only leave the panel out, sometimes chained or locked to a frame, or bring both home if the site has a known theft problem. Ask your allotment secretary whether the site has had break-ins recently — it should factor into whether you buy a permanently sited system or a fully portable one you carry in and out each visit.

### Can I run an extension lead from a neighbour's house to my allotment shed?

No. Aside from almost certainly breaching your tenancy agreement, running a domestic extension lead any meaningful distance outdoors is unsafe and explicitly incompatible with the plug-in microgenerator rules, which ban extension leads even for compliant kits used inside a single property. Battery-based portable power is the only sensible option for allotment sheds without their own mains supply.

### Will plug-in solar ever be legal for allotments specifically?

The current SI 2026/848 framework is built around domestic properties with an existing fused mains supply, so it wouldn't need separate allotment-specific legislation — a plot with a proper mains connection could technically host a compliant kit under the same rules as a house. The barrier isn't the law, it's that almost no UK allotment sites have mains electricity to individual plots in the first place, and that's an infrastructure and site-management decision rather than a regulatory one.

### Do I need permission from my allotment association to use a portable solar panel?

Most associations don't restrict portable, unwired equipment the way they restrict permanent electrical installations, but rules vary by site — some have specific policies on visible equipment, shed additions, or noise from inverters. A quick email to your site secretary before buying anything is worth the five minutes it takes.
