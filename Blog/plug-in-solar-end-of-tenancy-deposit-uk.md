Plug-in solar's biggest selling point for renters is that it's genuinely portable — the kit belongs to you, not the flat, and it comes with you when you move. That's a huge step forward from the "you can't justify solar if you're not staying" problem that's kept renewable energy out of rented homes for years. But portable isn't automatically risk-free. If you install a kit badly, or leave marks on a balcony rail, a garden fence or a brick wall when you take it down, you can still end up with a deposit deduction at the end of your tenancy — and deposit disputes over DIY changes are one of the most common reasons renters lose money through schemes like the Deposit Protection Service, MyDeposits and the Tenancy Deposit Scheme.

The good news is that avoiding this is almost entirely about how you install and remove the kit, not whether you're allowed to have it at all. Here's what actually matters.

## Start with permission, not installation

Most tenancy agreements say nothing specific about plug-in solar because the product didn't exist when the agreement was signed. That ambiguity cuts both ways: it means you're not automatically breaching your tenancy by installing a freestanding kit, but it also means a landlord could argue a wall-mounted or drilled installation counts as an "alteration" requiring consent under a standard clause. Since the [Renters' Rights Act 2025](/blog/renters-rights-act-2025-solar) strengthened tenant protections generally, most landlords are more receptive to a quick, written request than they were a few years ago — but get it in writing regardless, even if it's just a text message confirming they're fine with a ground-standing or clamp-mounted kit on the balcony rail. If your landlord refuses outright, our guide on [what to do when a landlord refuses plug-in solar](/blog/plug-in-solar-landlord-refused-what-next-uk) covers your options before you escalate anything.

## Choose a mounting method that leaves no trace

This is the single biggest factor in whether your deposit is affected. Drilling into brick, render or a balcony structure to mount brackets is the kind of change that shows up clearly on a check-out inventory and is hard to argue your way out of. The alternatives that avoid this entirely:

- **Ground-standing frames** for gardens and patios — nothing attached to any building surface at all.
- **Clamp-on balcony rail brackets** that grip the rail rather than drilling through it. Our [balcony solar installation guide](/blog/balcony-solar-installation-guide-uk) covers the clamp types that are genuinely reversible.
- **Weighted or ballasted bases** for flat roof terraces, which hold the panel in place through weight rather than fixings.

If you're in a flat specifically, our [best kits for flats guide](/blog/best-plug-in-solar-kits-for-flats-uk) is worth checking before you buy, since not every kit on the market ships with a no-drill mounting option as standard.

## Document the "before" state

Before you install anything, take dated photographs of the balcony, garden or wall space exactly as it is — the same thing you'd normally do for a check-in inventory, but focused specifically on the area your kit will occupy. This matters for two reasons: it proves the space was already in that condition if a landlord later tries to attribute unrelated wear to your installation, and it gives you a clear reference to restore the space to when you move out. Keep these photos somewhere you'll actually find them again in a year or two, not just on a phone you might upgrade.

## What actually causes deposit deductions

Deposit scheme adjudicators generally only deduct for damage beyond normal wear and tear, or for failing to return a property to its original condition. For plug-in solar specifically, the realistic risk areas are:

- **Fixing holes** from drilled brackets that weren't filled and made good before check-out.
- **Cable routing damage** — a cable run forced through a window seal, door frame or wall without proper weatherproofing can leave marks or cause damp issues over a tenancy.
- **Marks on balcony rails or paintwork** from clamps that weren't padded or were over-tightened.
- **Staining or corrosion** on a patio or decking from a ground-standing frame left in one place for a long tenancy, particularly in [coastal areas where salt corrosion](/blog/plug-in-solar-coastal-areas-salt-corrosion-uk) is a factor.

None of these are hard to avoid — they just require treating the installation as genuinely temporary from day one, rather than retrofitting that mindset when you're already packing boxes.

## The move-out process

When your tenancy ends, removal should take less time than installation did. Disconnect and pack the panel and inverter, remove any clamps or ground frames, fill and touch up any fixing holes if you used them (ideally avoid this entirely per the section above), and photograph the space again once everything's out. If you're moving straight into another rental or a home you own, see our guide on [taking your panels when you move house](/blog/taking-solar-panels-when-moving) for how to transport and reinstall the same kit at your new address — one of the only home-energy upgrades where "but I'm renting" was never actually a reason to wait.

### Do I need my landlord's permission to install plug-in solar?

Not always, but it depends on your tenancy agreement. A freestanding, ground-standing or clamp-mounted kit that doesn't alter the structure usually doesn't need consent, but check your agreement's clause on alterations and get anything ambiguous confirmed in writing to avoid a dispute later.

### Will plug-in solar affect my tenancy deposit?

Only if the installation causes actual damage — drilled holes that weren't made good, cable damage, or marks from poorly fitted clamps. A no-drill, freestanding or clamp-on setup that's removed carefully at the end of your tenancy shouldn't affect your deposit at all.

### What's the safest mounting option for renters?

Ground-standing frames and clamp-on balcony rail brackets are the lowest-risk options, since neither requires drilling into brick, render or balcony structure. Our [balcony installation guide](/blog/balcony-solar-installation-guide-uk) covers the specific bracket types to look for.
