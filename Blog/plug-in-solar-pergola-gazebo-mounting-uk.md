Garden pergolas and gazebos have become one of the more popular spots homeowners ask about when planning a [plug-in solar](/blog/plug-in-solar-uk-complete-guide) installation, and it's easy to see why. A pergola roof is usually unshaded, already elevated above head height, and — unlike a house roof — doesn't need [DNO notification](/blog/g98-dno-notification-step-by-step) paperwork to touch. But "can I put a panel up there" and "should I" aren't quite the same question, and the answer depends heavily on what your pergola is actually made of.

## Why pergolas look so tempting

Most UK gardens with a pergola or gazebo have already solved the two hardest problems in plug-in solar siting: shading and angle. A well-placed pergola sits clear of fences, hedges, and overhanging trees, and its flat or slightly pitched roof structure gives you a stable base to work from — something a [fence-mounted panel](/blog/fence-mounted-plug-in-solar-panels-uk) or ground-mounted frame doesn't always offer. If your garden office or patio is already built around a pergola, mounting solar there can also mean shorter cable runs to whatever you're trying to power, which matters given the [no-extension-lead rule](/blog/plug-in-solar-no-extension-lead-rule-uk).

The catch is structural. Timber pergolas, especially older or decorative ones, aren't built to the same load tolerances as a proper [flat roof mounting system](/blog/flat-roof-plug-in-solar-uk) or [ground mount frame](/blog/ground-mount-plug-in-solar). An 800W kit with two rigid panels and brackets can add 20–30kg of static weight plus wind load, and a lightweight decorative structure may simply not be rated for that.

## What actually works

**Solid timber pergolas with a cross-beam roof** are generally fine for lightweight mounting kits, provided the beams are at least 70mm x 70mm and in good condition — rotten or undersized beams are a hard no regardless of panel weight. Use roof-rated brackets rather than improvising with cable ties or builder's strap; the same [adjustable tilt brackets](/blog/best-adjustable-solar-panel-tilt-brackets-uk) used for flat roofs work well here because they let you angle panels toward the sun rather than following the pergola's flat line.

**Metal-framed gazebos and pergola kits** (aluminium or steel) tend to tolerate panel weight better than timber, but check the manufacturer's load rating before drilling or clamping anything — most garden gazebo kits are rated for snow load, not for a permanently mounted 10kg+ panel plus bracket hardware.

**Polycarbonate or fabric-roofed gazebos** are the one category to avoid. There's no structural surface to mount to, and panels need rigid, ventilated backing to avoid overheating — something a fabric canopy can't provide.

## Getting the angle right

Most pergolas are built flat or with a very shallow pitch, which is rarely the optimum angle for UK solar generation (30–40° facing south is generally best — see our guide on [how to angle solar panels for maximum output](/blog/how-to-angle-solar-panels-maximum-output-uk)). Rather than lay panels flush to a flat pergola roof, use adjustable brackets to tilt them up from the beam line. This does increase wind loading, so make sure brackets are rated for the tilt angle you're using and that fixings go into structural timber, not just decking boards or trim.

If your pergola runs east–west rather than north–south, read our [east vs west facing](/blog/east-west-facing-plug-in-solar-uk) guide before assuming a south-facing bracket angle will help — the beam orientation matters as much as the tilt.

## Cabling and the 5A fuse rule

Whatever structure you mount to, the same [800VA/3.5A](/blog/plug-in-solar-uk-complete-guide) rules apply: BS 1363 plug, dedicated outdoor socket, 5A fuse, and — critically — no extension leads between panel and socket. A pergola at the back of a long garden often tempts people into running a lead to the nearest indoor socket, which isn't compliant. If your pergola isn't within plug reach of a qualifying [outdoor socket](/blog/best-outdoor-socket-plug-in-solar-uk), that's a job for a qualified electrician to install a new outdoor circuit — not a workaround with a cable reel.

Weatherproofing matters more on an open-sided pergola than a walled garden office, since cable runs are more exposed to rain and wind. Our [weatherproofing accessories](/blog/plug-in-solar-weatherproofing-accessories-uk) guide covers IP-rated glands and connectors worth using here.

## How much power to expect

A well-sited pergola panel performs similarly to a comparable [ground mount](/blog/ground-mount-plug-in-solar) in the same garden — typically 1.5–3kWh per day in summer for an 800W kit with good south-facing exposure, dropping to well under 1kWh/day in midwinter. See our [how much power does a solar panel generate](/blog/how-much-power-solar-panel-generate-uk) guide for seasonal estimates by region. Partial shading from the pergola's own cross-beams can reduce output more than people expect, since even a thin shadow line across a panel edge can cut generation disproportionately — plan the mounting position to keep beam shadows off the panel face during peak sun hours.

### Do I need planning permission to mount solar on a pergola?

No — plug-in solar kits sit outside planning permission requirements in almost all cases, the same as any other garden-mounted installation. The exceptions are the usual ones: listed buildings and some conservation areas, covered in our [conservation areas guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-conservation-areas-uk).

### Will the extra weight damage my pergola over time?

It can, if the structure wasn't built for it. Have a look at the beam condition before fitting — rot, warping, or undersized timber are the real risks, not the panel weight itself on a well-built pergola. When in doubt, get a builder or structural opinion before drilling.

### Can I mount a battery unit on the pergola too?

No — the [no-battery-in-plug-circuit rule](/blog/plug-in-solar-no-battery-rule-uk-explained) means any battery storage has to sit separately from the plug-in circuit itself, typically on the ground or a shelf beneath the pergola rather than fixed to the roof structure.
