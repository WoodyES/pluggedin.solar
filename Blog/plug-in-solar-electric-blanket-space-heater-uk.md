As temperatures drop across the UK, a question we're hearing a lot from new plug-in solar owners is simple: can my kit actually run anything that keeps me warm? With gas and electricity prices still biting and the [Ofgem price cap](/blog/ofgem-price-cap-january-2027-plug-in-solar-uk) sitting close to 24.5p/kWh for most households this winter, it's a reasonable thing to want from a system you've already paid for. The honest answer is nuanced: an electric blanket, yes, comfortably. A space heater, only in the loosest, most occasional sense — and understanding why comes down to how [SI 2026 No. 848](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk) actually works, not just what your kit is physically capable of generating.

## Why generation and consumption don't line up in winter

A legally compliant plug-in microgenerator kit is capped at 800VA/3.5A output, which sounds like plenty next to a 60W electric blanket. But that figure is a ceiling on what the panel and inverter can ever push through the socket — not what they're actually producing on a grey November afternoon. Short days, a low sun angle, and far more cloud cover mean a typical 800W setup is realistically producing a fraction of its summer output right now. Our [winter preparation guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-winter-preparation-2026-uk) sets expectations properly: many owners see generation drop to well under half of what the same panels managed in June and July, and on heavily overcast days output can fall close to zero for hours at a stretch.

That matters because plug-in solar in the UK has no permitted battery in the circuit. Under the no-battery rule, every watt your panel produces has to be used in your home the instant it's generated or it's lost — there's nothing banking daytime surplus for a 7pm top-up. Our [no-battery rule explainer](/blog/plug-in-solar-no-battery-rule-uk-explained) covers exactly why the regulations are written this way, but the practical upshot for winter evenings is blunt: by the time you're reaching for a blanket or heater after dark, your plug-in kit has stopped generating for the day entirely. It isn't a backup power source. It's a daytime offset against whatever else is running in the house.

## Electric blankets: a genuinely good match

An electric blanket is one of the best-suited appliances for a plug-in solar kit, just not for the reason most people assume. A typical single electric blanket draws somewhere between 40W and 100W, well inside what even a modest kit can supply during daylight hours. If you're someone who naps, works from a sofa, or simply likes a pre-warmed bed made up mid-afternoon, running a blanket off daytime generation is a straightforward, low-draw win. The catch is timing: run it in the evening after sunset and you're drawing from the grid exactly as you would anyway, with the solar panel doing nothing. The genuine saving comes from matching low-power appliances to daylight hours, which our [best appliances to run guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-best-appliances-to-run-uk) goes through appliance by appliance.

## Space heaters are a different story

This is where expectations need resetting. A typical portable electric space heater draws 1,000–2,500W — three times the maximum your plug-in kit is legally allowed to output, even before accounting for winter's reduced generation. There is no compliant plug-in solar configuration that will run a space heater on its own; the 800VA/3.5A cap under the current specification simply isn't built for heating loads of that size. At best, on a bright winter afternoon, your panel might offset a modest fraction of a heater's draw while the rest comes from the grid as normal — but it won't meaningfully reduce the bill impact of running one for an evening.

If home heating cost is really what you're trying to solve, plug-in solar isn't the right tool for the job; it's suited to lower-draw continuous loads like fridges, routers, and — as covered above — blankets, not high-wattage heating appliances. Some owners instead look at an [immersion heater](/blog/plug-in-solar-immersion-heater-uk) setup, which can make better use of daytime surplus for hot water rather than space heating, though the same 800VA ceiling still applies.

## Making the most of what you've got this winter

The best strategy for winter is shifting your own habits to match the kit rather than expecting the kit to match your habits. Run the washing machine, dishwasher, or a blanket warm-up in the early afternoon when output peaks, rather than after dark. Checking your app's live output before switching anything on is the single most effective free upgrade you can make to a plug-in solar setup in December. It's also worth double-checking your unit is still listed on the ENA's [compliant devices registry](/blog/uk-compliant-plug-in-solar-devices-live) — the list is refreshed regularly, and any kit bought secondhand or from an unfamiliar retailer is worth verifying before winter puts it under heavier daily use. If you haven't yet filed your [G98 notification](/blog/g98-notification-guide-uk-homeowners), this is also a good moment to confirm it's done, since most DNOs process these quickest outside the summer rush. For anyone shopping for a second unit before spring, our [retailers guide](/blog/retailers-stocking-plug-in-solar-uk-2026) covers current UK stock levels at B&Q, Currys, Screwfix and Wickes.

### Can I run a space heater directly from my plug-in solar panel?

No. Compliant plug-in kits are capped at 800VA/3.5A, well under the 1,000–2,500W a typical space heater draws, and the UK's no-battery rule means there's nothing stored for evening use once generation stops at dusk.

### Does an electric blanket run well on plug-in solar?

Yes — most electric blankets draw only 40–100W, comfortably within an 800W kit's output. The saving only applies when you run it during daylight hours; after sunset, your home draws from the grid as normal since plug-in solar has no battery.

### Why can't plug-in solar kits include a battery for evening heating?

The current UK specification under SI 2026 No. 848 prohibits a battery in the plug circuit for safety and certification reasons. Some owners pair a separate, non-connected battery system for evening use instead — see our guide on [evening battery storage](/blog/best-solar-battery-evening-use-uk) for how that works alongside (not inside) a compliant kit.
