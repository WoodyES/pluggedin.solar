Since [SI 2026 No. 848](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk) made "plug-in microgenerators" a recognised legal category on 27 August 2026, most of the country has treated buying one the same way as buying a kettle — plug it in, notify your DNO, done. England and Wales's fifteen National Parks (and the National Landscapes formerly known as AONBs) are the exception that keeps coming up in reader questions, because planning culture inside their boundaries is genuinely different, even if the electrical rules under G98 aren't. If you live inside the Lake District, Peak District, Dartmoor, Snowdonia/Eryri or the Yorkshire Dales, here's what actually changes and what doesn't.

## The electrical rules don't change at all

Start with what's constant: a plug-in solar kit's compliance obligations are identical whether you live in central Leeds or in the middle of the Yorkshire Dales National Park. You still need a micro-inverter listed on the [ENA's G98 compliant device register](/blog/uk-compliant-plug-in-solar-devices-live), you still need to stay within the 800VA/3.5A output limit, you still need a standard BS 1363 plug into a dedicated outlet behind its own 5A fuse, and you still need to notify your [DNO](/blog/g98-notification-guide-uk-homeowners) before switching it on. National Park status has zero bearing on any of that — it's a planning and visual-amenity question, not an electrical safety one.

## Where it gets different: permitted development rights

Ordinarily, small household solar installations in England benefit from "permitted development" rights, meaning you don't need to apply for planning permission at all provided the kit stays within set limits. Inside National Parks and National Landscapes, those permitted development rights are narrower. The general principle — sometimes called the Sandford Principle — is that where conservation of the landscape and public enjoyment conflict, conservation takes priority, and local planning authorities inside these designated areas apply that principle to visible structures far more strictly than an equivalent authority would in an ordinary suburb.

In practice, this mostly bites on *fixed, roof-mounted* solar installations, where panels projecting above the roof slope or visible from a public highway can trigger a need for planning consent inside a National Park even though the identical installation would be permitted development outside one. A genuine plug-in kit sitting on a [ground frame](/blog/ground-mount-plug-in-solar) in a back garden, strapped to a [balcony rail](/blog/balcony-rail-mounting-plug-in-solar), or mounted flat against a rear fence is a much lower-risk case — it isn't a building operation, and most National Park planning authorities won't ask for anything provided it isn't prominent from a public road, footpath or viewpoint. The risk rises sharply if you're in a conservation area *within* a National Park, a listed building, or proposing anything visible from the front of the property — our guides on [conservation areas](/blog/plug-in-solar-conservation-areas-uk) and [listed buildings](/blog/plug-in-solar-listed-buildings-uk) cover the general principles that apply doubly hard inside park boundaries.

## Article 4 Directions are the thing to actually check

The detail that trips people up is the Article 4 Direction — a mechanism local planning authorities use to withdraw specific permitted development rights in a defined area, more aggressively and more commonly used inside National Parks than almost anywhere else. An Article 4 Direction covering your street or hamlet can mean that even a modest, low-visibility installation that would sail through elsewhere needs a full planning application here. Every National Park Authority publishes its Article 4 Directions, usually as a searchable map on its own planning pages, and it's a five-minute check worth doing before you buy anything, not after. If in doubt, a quick call or email to the Park Authority's planning duty officer — not your local district council, which usually isn't the decision-maker inside park boundaries — is the fastest way to get a definitive answer for your specific property.

## Which National Parks see the most questions

Three come up repeatedly in reader correspondence. The **Lake District** has some of the tightest controls in the country given its World Heritage Site status alongside National Park designation — anything visible from a fell path or lakeside is scrutinised closely. **Dartmoor**, covering a big chunk of our [Devon guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-devon-guide-uk) area, has similarly strict rules around its open moorland and traditional stone-walled settlements. The **Yorkshire Dales**, within the wider area we cover in our [Yorkshire guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-yorkshire-guide-uk), sees less friction in practice because many villages there have gardens and yards tucked behind buildings, out of direct sightline from the main approach roads — but the Article 4 check still applies.

## What to actually do before you buy

- Confirm whether your specific address sits inside a National Park, a National Landscape, or neither — park boundaries often follow field and parish lines rather than obvious geography, so don't assume based on the nearest town name
- Search your Park Authority's online Article 4 Direction map for your postcode or parish
- Choose a kit you can mount somewhere low-visibility — rear garden, enclosed yard, or a fence not facing a road or footpath — rather than a front-facing wall or visible roof slope
- Check our [best plug-in solar kit](/blog/best-plug-in-solar-kit-uk) roundup for ground- and fence-mountable options that keep your options open if a planning query does come up
- Still complete your G98 DNO notification regardless of planning status — the two processes are entirely separate

### Do I need planning permission for a plug-in solar kit inside a National Park?

Usually not, if it's a small, low-visibility installation — a ground- or fence-mounted kit in a back garden, out of sight from public roads and footpaths. The risk rises for anything roof-mounted, front-facing, or inside an area covered by an Article 4 Direction withdrawing normal permitted development rights, which National Park Authorities use more often than ordinary councils.

### Does National Park status affect the G98 electrical compliance rules?

No. The G98 DNO notification requirement, the 800VA/3.5A output limit, and every other electrical rule under SI 2026 No. 848 apply identically everywhere in Great Britain. National Park rules only affect whether you need separate planning permission for the physical installation itself.

### Who do I actually ask if I'm not sure whether my property needs consent?

Contact the relevant National Park Authority's planning department directly rather than your local district or borough council — National Park Authorities are usually the statutory planning decision-maker for land inside their boundary, and their duty planning officers can give a quick, property-specific answer.
