If you installed a plug-in solar kit in the launch rush after [SI 2026 No. 848 made it legal on 27 August](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk), November is the first month where the app numbers stop looking like a solar system and start looking like a very small trickle charger. Daylight in the UK drops to under 8 hours a day by month-end, the sun barely clears rooftop height at midday, and low cloud sits over most of the country for days at a time. That's normal. Here's what to actually expect, and how to get the most out of the panels you've got until spring generation returns.

## What an 800W system actually produces in November

Across the UK, an 800W plug-in kit typically produces somewhere between 0.5 and 1.0 kWh per day in November, depending on latitude, cloud cover and panel orientation. That's roughly a third of what the same system generated in September, covered in our [September generation guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-september-generation-uk), and it will drop further before January — see the [January output guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-january-uk-generation) for the full-winter picture.

| Region | Daily average (kWh) | Monthly total (kWh) |
|---|---|---|
| South England | 0.9–1.1 | 27–33 |
| Midlands | 0.8–1.0 | 24–30 |
| North England | 0.7–0.9 | 21–27 |
| Wales | 0.75–0.95 | 22–28 |
| Scotland | 0.5–0.8 | 15–24 |

These are PVGIS-typical figures for a well-sited, unshaded 800W south-facing array. Overcast, east/west-facing or partially shaded installs will sit at the lower end or below it. Use the site [calculator](/calculator) to model your own postcode and orientation rather than relying on regional averages.

## Why November is worse than it looks on paper

The clocks changed at the end of October — see our [clocks-change guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-october-clocks-change-uk) — so peak generation now falls squarely inside the working day for most households, roughly 10:30am to 2:30pm. That sounds like it should help self-consumption, but November also brings the year's heaviest persistent cloud cover in most regions, plus low sun angles that increase the chance of shading from neighbouring buildings, fences and even bare winter trees that weren't a problem in summer. A panel that had clear sky access in July can pick up two or three extra hours of shade in November purely from the sun's lower arc.

If your generation looks unusually low even accounting for the season, check the basics before assuming a fault: cable connections, panel angle, and whether autumn leaf fall or bird mess has built up on the glass. Our general [winter preparation guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-winter-preparation-2026-uk) covers a proper pre-winter checklist, and if you're still seeing numbers that don't add up after that, the [troubleshooting guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-not-generating-troubleshooting-uk) walks through fault-finding step by step.

## Getting the angle right for low winter sun

Most plug-in kits ship with an adjustable tilt bracket, and November is exactly when it's worth using it. At UK latitudes, a steeper angle — 50–60° from horizontal rather than the 30–35° that suits summer — tracks the low winter sun arc far more efficiently. It's a five-minute adjustment that can meaningfully lift daily output for the rest of winter. If your bracket doesn't hold a steep enough angle, that's worth fixing now rather than accepting reduced output through to February.

## Should you bother with a battery-paired system in November?

With generation this low, a battery adds less value in November than it did in September — there's often barely enough surplus to fill even a small battery on a cloudy day. That said, on the clearer days (and the UK does still get some crisp, cold, sunny November days with excellent panel output despite the cold) a battery lets you bank the midday surplus for evening use rather than exporting it for nothing under most plug-in setups. If you haven't yet worked out whether a battery pays for itself at your usage pattern, our [battery vs no battery comparison](/blog/battery-storage-vs-no-battery-plug-in-solar-uk) breaks down the economics properly.

## Setting expectations for the rest of winter

November's numbers will look thin, but they're not a sign anything's wrong — they're the trough before the long, slow climb back that starts properly in March. Keep the panels clean, keep the angle steep, and don't be tempted to disconnect anything over winter: even 0.5 kWh a day is a kettle boil or two you're not paying grid rates for, and it costs nothing to keep collecting it.

### Is 0.5 kWh a day even worth having panels connected for?

Yes — at the Ofgem price cap of roughly 24.5p/kWh, 0.5 kWh a day is worth around £45 over the month, and it comes at zero marginal cost once the kit is installed. The real value of plug-in solar was always the annual total, not any single month.

### Should I reposition my panels for winter or leave them where they are?

If your bracket allows a steeper angle without needing to unmount the panel, adjust it — it's free output. If repositioning means taking the panel down and remounting elsewhere, it's rarely worth it for winter alone; focus on angle rather than location.

### Will a dirty panel make a bigger difference in November than in summer?

Proportionally, yes. Because November's light is already weak and often diffuse through cloud, a layer of grime or wet leaves blocks a larger share of the available light than it would on a bright July day. A quick wipe-down after autumn leaf fall is worth doing.
