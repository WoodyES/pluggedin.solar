Plug-in solar has only been legal in the UK since [27 August 2026](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk), but the first wave of kits is already old enough that some owners are dealing with a cracked panel, a dead micro-inverter, or simply the urge to [upgrade to a bigger kit](/blog/expanding-plug-in-solar-system-uk) before winter. Unlike a broken kettle, solar hardware doesn't belong in a general waste bin — it's covered by specific electronic waste rules, and getting rid of it the wrong way can mean a fine from your local council as well as a wasted set of genuinely recoverable materials.

## Why a solar panel can't just go in the bin

A standard plug-in solar panel is mostly glass and aluminium by weight, with copper wiring and a junction box containing small amounts of silver and other metals. Panels fall under the UK's Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment (WEEE) regulations, which classify them as electronic waste regardless of how they're wired into your home. That means they're banned from kerbside general waste and must go through a registered WEEE recycling route — either a local authority recycling centre that accepts panels, a retailer take-back scheme, or a specialist solar recycler.

The micro-inverter is covered by the same WEEE rules but is handled differently in practice, since it's a small electronic device rather than a bulky glass panel. If you've previously had to troubleshoot one using our guide to [micro-inverter error codes](/blog/micro-inverter-error-codes-uk), you'll know the unit itself is sealed — don't attempt to open it for "disposal" either, since a damaged sealed unit can leak more easily and most councils want it intact for safe processing.

## Where to actually take it

**Household Waste Recycling Centres (HWRCs).** Most council-run tips now accept solar panels and small electronics as part of their WEEE stream, but policies vary by local authority — some charge a small fee for panels over a certain size, others take them free. Phone ahead or check your council's website before loading a panel into the car, since turning up unannounced with something your local site doesn't stock-handle is a wasted trip.

**Retailer take-back schemes.** Under WEEE rules, UK retailers selling electrical goods — including [B&Q, Currys, Screwfix and Wickes](/blog/retailers-stocking-plug-in-solar-uk-2026) — are required to offer free take-back of equivalent old items when you buy a replacement. If you're swapping an old kit for a new one from the same retailer, ask about this before you dispose of the old unit yourself.

**Manufacturer recycling programmes.** Several solar and portable power brands run their own take-back or recycling partnerships for end-of-life hardware, separate from the standard WEEE retailer obligation. Worth checking with whoever made your kit before defaulting to the council tip, particularly for premium kits still well within their [warranty period](/blog/plug-in-solar-warranty-guide-uk) — a fault that looks like "time to recycle it" might actually be a valid claim instead.

## Before you recycle, check it isn't a warranty or insurance matter

It's easy to assume a panel producing noticeably less output than it used to is simply worn out, but [degradation rates](/blog/solar-panel-degradation-rate-uk) for a panel this new should be minimal — a sudden drop is more often a loose connector, shading, or a fault that's still covered. Before taking anything to a recycling centre:

- Check whether the panel or inverter is still inside its manufacturer warranty window
- Confirm you've told your home insurer about the system, since a [fire-risk or damage claim](/blog/plug-in-solar-fire-risk-insurance-uk) may cover a genuinely faulty unit
- Take photos of the fault and serial numbers before you dismantle anything, in case you need to make a claim later

## Buying compliant gear in the first place reduces the problem later

A lot of the recycling headaches we hear about trace back to kits that were never properly [G98 DNO-notified](/blog/g98-notification-guide-uk-homeowners) or compliant with the microgenerator spec in the first place — cheap, unbranded units with no clear manufacturer support line and no recycling programme to speak of. Before buying, it's worth cross-checking a model against the [live register of ENA-compliant plug-in solar devices](/blog/uk-compliant-plug-in-solar-devices-live), which lists products that have gone through the formal compliance process and are far more likely to have a proper end-of-life route when the time eventually comes. Our [guide to spotting a non-compliant kit](/blog/how-to-spot-non-compliant-plug-in-solar-kit-uk) covers the warning signs if you're not sure about a listing you've already bought.

## What happens to the materials

Commercial panel recycling in the UK typically recovers the aluminium frame and the bulk of the glass — together the majority of a panel's weight — along with copper wiring from the junction box and cabling. The silicon cells themselves, along with trace silver and other rare materials, are harder to recover economically with current UK processing capacity, so a meaningful share of a panel's embedded value is still lost rather than fully reclaimed. This is improving as volumes increase, but it's worth knowing that "recycled" currently means the bulk materials are diverted from landfill, not that every component is reclaimed.

### Can I put a dead plug-in solar panel in my household recycling bin?

No. Solar panels fall under WEEE electronic waste rules and must go to a registered recycling point — a council HWRC that accepts panels, a retailer take-back scheme, or a manufacturer recycling programme. They're banned from general kerbside waste.

### Is my old plug-in solar kit covered by a retailer take-back scheme?

If you bought it from a retailer covered by WEEE take-back obligations — including [B&Q, Currys, Screwfix and Wickes](/blog/retailers-stocking-plug-in-solar-uk-2026) — you're entitled to free take-back of an equivalent old item when buying a replacement. Ask in-store or check the retailer's WEEE policy before disposing of it yourself.

### What should I check before recycling a panel that's underperforming?

Confirm it's genuinely faulty rather than shaded, loose-wired, or affected by normal [seasonal variation](/blog/solar-panel-output-winter-vs-summer-uk), then check whether it's still within its manufacturer warranty or covered under your home insurance before recycling it — a premature claim beats a premature trip to the tip.
