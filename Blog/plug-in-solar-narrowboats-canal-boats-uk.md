Since plug-in solar became legal on 27 August 2026 under SI 2026 No. 848, we've had a steady trickle of questions from a group we didn't expect: narrowboat owners. If you've got a residential mooring with a shoreline power pedestal that ends in a standard UK socket, it's a fair question — can you just plug in an 800W kit the same way a house does?

The short answer is: sometimes, but usually not, and there's almost always a better option for a boat anyway. Here's the detail.

## What the "plug-in microgenerator" rules actually cover

The new legal category — a [plug-in microgenerator](/blog/plug-in-microgenerator-uk-what-is-it) — is defined around a domestic property's fixed wiring. [SI 2026 No. 848](/blog/si-2026-848-plug-in-solar-explained-uk) permits a maximum 800VA/3.5A unit to be plugged into a standard BS 1363 13A socket on a **household's own electrical installation**, with a 5A fuse in the plug, no battery in the circuit, and — critically — [no extension leads](/blog/plug-in-solar-no-extension-lead-rule-uk) between the panel and the wall socket. One unit per household. Full detail on the legal position is in our [is plug-in solar legal in the UK](/blog/is-plug-in-solar-legal-uk) guide.

That wording matters for boats. A marina pedestal socket isn't part of your boat's internal wiring — it's the marina's supply, metered and often shared across a ring main serving dozens of berths. The regulation was written with a homeowner's consumer unit in mind, not a third-party shore power connection, so it doesn't cleanly apply to a boat plugged into a marina bollard. Some marinas may permit it under their own site rules; many won't, because a microgenerator feeding back into a shared shore supply raises questions their electrical certification wasn't designed to answer. If you're on a residential mooring and want to try it, the marina operator's permission is the first call to make, not an afterthought — treat it the same way a [leasehold flat](/blog/leasehold-flat-solar-permission-uk) resident would treat freeholder consent.

There's a second, more basic issue: most narrowboats don't run on mains electricity when cruising or moored away from a hookup at all. They run on a 12V (sometimes 24V) domestic battery bank charged by the engine alternator, and increasingly, solar. Plugging an 800W mains microgenerator into a shore socket only helps on the days you're actually connected to shore power — which for many boaters is a minority of the year.

## The setup that actually works for most boats

For continuous cruisers, liveaboards without permanent shore power, or anyone wanting solar that works whether they're moored or on the move, the answer isn't a plug-in kit at all — it's the same flexible panel and DC battery setup that's long been standard in the boating and camping world, just improved by the same battery and monitoring tech driving the plug-in solar boom on land.

A practical narrowboat solar setup typically means:

- **Flexible or semi-flexible panels** bonded to the cabin roof, sized to the available flat space (often 200-400W total across one or two panels) — see our [best flexible solar panels](/blog/best-flexible-solar-panels-uk-caravans) roundup, written for caravans but equally applicable to boat roofs.
- **An MPPT charge controller** wired directly into the boat's 12V/24V leisure battery bank, not a mains inverter setup.
- **A portable power station** as a flexible middle ground for boats without a fixed leisure battery installation, or as backup capacity for electronics, laptops and lighting.

This is a genuinely different system to the plug-in microgenerator kits we cover for houses and flats, and it's worth being clear about that distinction so you don't buy the wrong thing. Our [caravans and motorhomes guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-caravans-motorhomes-uk) covers the same DC-coupled approach in more depth, since the electrical logic is nearly identical to a boat.

## If you do have shore power and want to try a plug-in kit

If your mooring contract explicitly allows it and the marina confirms your pedestal is on its own protected circuit, a plug-in microgenerator can still offset some daytime shore power draw — running a fridge, battery charger or dehumidifier while you're plugged in. Stick to the same rules a house would follow: one unit, factory lead only, mounted so cabling isn't run across the towpath or gunwale as a trip hazard, and don't assume it replaces your leisure battery charging on days you're not connected. Our general [buying checklist](/blog/plug-in-solar-buying-checklist-uk) is a reasonable starting point for evaluating kits, but read it alongside your marina's electrical policy, not instead of it.

Off-grid cabin owners face a similar mains-vs-DC decision, and our [off-grid cabins guide](/blog/plug-in-solar-off-grid-cabins-uk) walks through the same trade-off in a static context if you want the reasoning laid out in more detail.

## Product reliability for marine use

Whichever route you take, buy for the marine environment, not the garden. Look for panels rated IP67 or IP68, stainless or marine-grade fixings if bonding permanently to a steel roof, and cabling routed to avoid standing water in the well deck. Our [EcoFlow 400W portable panel review](/blog/ecoflow-400w-portable-panel-review-uk) covers one option that's held up well in wet-weather testing and folds down small enough for boat storage — a real consideration when locker space is measured in inches, not feet.

### Can I plug an EcoFlow STREAM kit into my marina's shore power socket?

Only with your marina's explicit permission, and only if their pedestal supply is confirmed suitable — the legal plug-in microgenerator framework was written around domestic household wiring, not shared marina shore power circuits, so it's a grey area the regulation doesn't directly address. Check with your marina operator before buying.

### What's the difference between a plug-in solar kit and a boat solar setup?

A plug-in kit feeds AC power directly into a household's mains wiring through a standard socket. A boat setup uses flexible panels wired through an MPPT controller straight into a 12V/24V DC leisure battery bank — no mains connection at all, which is why it works whether you're cruising, wild mooring, or plugged into shore power.

### How much solar do I need for a narrowboat?

Most liveaboards find 200-400W of roof-mounted flexible panel enough to keep a leisure battery bank topped up for lighting, water pump, and small electronics through spring to autumn, though a diesel or engine-driven charge is still typically needed to cover fridge and winter heating loads on shorter, duller days.
